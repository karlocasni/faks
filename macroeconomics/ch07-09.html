1Must-know sheet

Ch 7 · Labor market

\(L = N + U\), \(u = U/L\), participation \(= L/\text{pop}\), employment rate \(= N/\text{pop}\)

WS: \(W = P^e F(u,z)\) → \(W/P = F(u,z)\), slopes down

PS: \(P = (1+m)W\) → \(W/P = 1/(1+m)\), flat

Equilibrium: \(F(u_n,z) = 1/(1+m)\)

z↑ (benefits, min. wage, protection): WS up → un↑, W/P same

m↑ (less competition, oil): PS down → un↑, W/P↓

Ch 8 · Phillips curve

\(\pi_t = \pi_t^e + (m+z) - \alpha u_t\)

\(u_n = (m+z)/\alpha\) ⇔ \(\pi - \pi^e = -\alpha(u - u_n)\)

θ = 0 (anchored): u below un → π higher but constant

θ = 1 (\(\pi^e = \pi_{t-1}\)): \(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + (m + z - \alpha u_t)\) → π rises every year

Indexation λ: \(\Delta\pi = -\frac{\alpha}{1-\lambda}(u - u_n)\), so divide the push by (1 − λ)

Ch 9 · IS-LM-PC

IS: \(Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y, r+x) + G\)

LM: \(r = \bar r\) (the bank sets the real rate)

PC: \(\pi - \bar\pi = (\alpha/L)(Y - Y_n)\)

Y > Yn → π above target; Y < Yn → below

Medium run: Y = Yn, r = rn, π = π̄. Money is neutral

Demand shock: bank moves r, Y back to Yn. Supply shock (oil): Yn itself falls

P1Problem 1 · The labor market (11 points)

EXAM STYLE

Croatia's labor market and the WS-PS model

Working-age population 2.8 million, employed 1.65 million, unemployed 140,000.
  1. Calculate the labor force, the participation rate, the unemployment rate and the employment rate. (2 points)
  2. Write the wage-setting and price-setting relations. Assuming P = Pᵉ, draw the model. Which two variables does it determine? (3 points)
  3. The markup is 5% and wage setting is W = P(1 − u). Calculate the natural rate. What happens if the markup rises to 10%? (3 points)
  4. Show the effect of an increase in the minimum wage in the WS-PS model and give one economic argument. (2 points)
  5. Why does the WS curve slope down? (1 point)
What to write
a

L = 1.65 + 0.14 = 1.79 m; participation = 1.79/2.8 = 63.9%; u = 0.14/1.79 = 7.8%; employment rate = 1.65/2.8 = 58.9%.

b

WS: \(W = P^e F(u,z)\); PS: \(P = (1+m)W\). With P = Pᵉ: WS \(W/P = F(u,z)\), PS \(W/P = 1/(1+m)\). Graph: u on the x-axis, W/P on the y-axis, WS sloping down, PS flat at 1/(1+m), crossing at A. The model determines the real wage and the natural rate un.

c

\(1 - u_n = 1/1.05 = 0.952\) → un = 4.76%. With m = 10%: \(1 - u_n = 0.909\) → un = 9.09%. A higher markup lowers the real wage (PS down). Workers accept the lower real wage only with more unemployment.

d

z↑ → WS shifts up → un↑, W/P unchanged. Argument: wage demands rise at every u, but the markup caps the real wage, so unemployment must rise to bring wage demands back down (firms hire fewer low-productivity workers).

e

Higher u weakens workers' bargaining power (it is harder to find another job) and lets firms pay a lower wage without losing workers, so the real wage falls.

Variants of part d to be ready forLower benefits: WS down, un↓. More competition (antitrust): m↓, PS up, W/P↑, un↓. Mergers or oil: m↑, PS down, W/P↓, un↑. Real wage for m = 0.1/0.2/0.3/0.4: 0.909 / 0.833 / 0.769 / 0.714.

P2Problem 2 · The Phillips curve (12 points)

EXAM STYLE

Expectations, the natural rate and indexation

The Phillips curve is \(\pi_t - \pi_t^e = 0.1 - 2u_t\), with \(\pi_t^e = \theta\pi_{t-1}\).
  1. Calculate the natural rate of unemployment. (1 point)
  2. θ = 0. The government cuts u to 3% in year t and keeps it there. Calculate inflation in t, t+1 and t+5. (2 points)
  3. From year t+5, θ = 1. Calculate inflation in t+5, t+6, t+7. Why might θ rise? (3 points)
  4. Now θ = 1, πt−1 = 0, u = 4% for good, and half of all labor contracts are indexed. Write the new Phillips curve and calculate inflation for t … t+3, with and without indexation. (3 points)
  5. Draw the curves from part d and mark the natural rate. (2 points)
  6. What happens to inflation when u < un? (1 point)
What to write
a

π = πᵉ: \(0 = 0.1 - 2u_n\) → un = 5%

b

πᵉ = 0, so \(\pi = 0.1 - 0.06\) → 4% in t, t+1 and t+5. Anchored expectations: lower u buys a higher but constant inflation rate.

c

\(\pi_t = \pi_{t-1} + 0.04\), starting from 4%: 8%, 12%, 16%. Why: inflation was 4% for years while people expected 0, so they learn and start using last year's inflation. Expectations de-anchor (the US in the 1970s).

d

Without indexation: \(\Delta\pi = 0.1 - 0.08 = 0.02\) → 2, 4, 6, 8%.

λ = 0.5: \(\pi_t - [0.5\pi_t + 0.5\pi_{t-1}] = 0.1 - 2u\) → πt − πt−1 = 0.2 − 4ut → \(\Delta\pi = 0.04\) → 4, 8, 12, 16%.

e
f

\(\pi - \pi^e = -\alpha(u - u_n) > 0\): inflation is above expected. With θ = 1 it rises every year. un is the NAIRU.

The other numeric setup they use\(\pi_t = 3.6\% - 0.2u_t\) (π̄ = 2%, m + z = 1.6%). u = 12/10/8/6/3% → π = 1.2/1.6/2.0/2.4/3.0%, so un = 8%. Holding u = 3% gives 3% every year. If πᵉ = πt−1: \(\Delta\pi = 1.6 - 0.2 \times 3 = +1\) point a year (3 → 4 → 5 → 6 → 7 → 8%). With \(\pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = -1.5(u_t - u_n)\), un = 8% and πt−1 = 4%, each point of u below 8% adds 1.5 points: u = 7/6/5/4% → 5.5/7/8.5/10%.

P3Problem 3 · IS-LM-PC (12 points)

EXAM STYLE

Shocks in the short and the medium run

The economy starts in medium-run equilibrium; πᵉ is anchored at the central bank's target π̄.
  1. Write the IS, LM and PC relations and describe the medium-run equilibrium. What is rn? (2 points)
  2. The government cuts its budget deficit. Show the short-run and medium-run effects. What happens to C and I? (4 points)
  3. The price of oil rises permanently. Show and explain the short and medium run. (3 points)
  4. A deep recession: i = 0 and π = −1%. What happens, and what policy helps? (2 points)
  5. rn = 2% and the risk premium x rises from 3% to 5%. What must the central bank do? (1 point)
What to write
a

IS: \(Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y, r+x) + G\); LM: \(r = \bar r\); PC: \(\pi - \bar\pi = (\alpha/L)(Y - Y_n)\). Medium run: Y = Yn, u = un, π = π̄, r = rn, the natural (neutral, Wicksellian) rate at which IS gives Yn; \(i = r_n + \bar\pi\).

b

Short run: G↓ or T↑ → IS left. At unchanged r, Y falls below Yn (A′) and π falls below target. C↓ (lower income), I↓ (lower sales).

Medium run: the bank cuts r to r′n (LM down), Y returns to Yn along IS′ (A″), π back to π̄. Result: same Y, lower r, higher I.

c

Oil works like a higher markup: m↑ → un↑ → Yn↓, so the PC shifts left. Short run: output is now above the new Y′n, so π rises. Medium run: the bank raises r (LM up), Y falls to Y′n, and π returns to target. On the way: stagflation. In the end: lower Y, higher un and r, lower real wage.

d

\(r = i - \pi^e = 0 - (-1\%) = 1\%\): the bank cannot lower r to rn. Output stays below Yn, deflation deepens, r rises, and output falls further: a deflation spiral. Fix: fiscal expansion (G↑, T↓) shifts IS right to Yn.

e

Keep r + x at 5%: cut r from 2% to 0% (LM down by 2 points; i falls by 2 points). The natural rate itself has fallen.

Other shocks, same recipeOverheating (r too low): raise r to rn, move along IS and the PC back to Yn. If ignored, expectations de-anchor and inflation accelerates. G↑: IS right, so raise r, and I falls by ΔG (crowding out). Recession: cut r (LM down) or G↑/T↓ (IS right). Policy smooths cycles, but Yn is uncertain, there are lags, and the ZLB binds.

★Flashcards · 1-point "explain" questions

Tap a question to reveal the answer. Say the answer out loud before you open it.

Why is the unemployment rate a poor measure of joblessness?

Discouraged workers stop searching, count as out of the labor force, and so lower u.

Unemployment rate vs participation rate?

u = U / labor force. Participation = labor force / working-age population.

Why does the nominal wage depend on Pᵉ, not P?

Wages are set before prices are known, and workers care about the real wage.

What is in z?

Everything that raises wages at given u and Pᵉ: unemployment benefits, employment protection, minimum wage, union power.

Why is PS horizontal?

With Y = N, firms set P = (1 + m)W whatever u is, so the real wage they imply, 1/(1+m), does not depend on u.

Efficiency wages?

Firms pay above the reservation wage to cut quits and raise productivity and morale (Henry Ford's $5 a day).

Natural rate of unemployment?

The u where the WS real wage equals the PS real wage (P = Pᵉ). It depends only on m and z: \(u_n = (m+z)/\alpha\).

Why did the Phillips curve "disappear" in the 1970s?

Inflation became persistent, so people started expecting last year's inflation (θ → 1). Low u then raised the change in inflation, not its level.

Friedman–Phelps?

The trade-off is only temporary: u stays below un only with ever-rising, unexpected inflation.

Why does indexation steepen the curve?

Indexed wages react to current inflation, so the wage-price spiral runs within the same year: slope α/(1 − λ).

Why does the curve break down at low inflation?

Workers resist nominal wage cuts (Portugal 2012), so high u stops pushing inflation down once wage growth reaches zero.

Why does un differ across countries and over time?

Labor-market institutions (benefits, protection, minimum wages, bargaining) and competition (the markup). Also demographics, temp agencies, disability.

Okun's law?

\(u - u_{-1} = -0.4(g_Y - 3\%)\): growth above about 3% lowers u by only 0.4 per point (labor hoarding, discouraged workers return).

Neutrality of money?

In the medium run monetary policy cannot change Y, u or r. It only sets inflation and the nominal rate.

What is the deflation spiral?

At i = 0, deflation raises r = −π. Higher r lowers output, which deepens deflation, and so on (Great Depression).

Why is an oil shock like a higher markup, and why were the 2000s milder?

Oil is a non-labor cost, so prices rise relative to wages. The 2000s were milder because of weaker bargaining power and better-anchored expectations.

Chapter seven

The Labor Market

IS-LM held the price level fixed: that is the short run. Now we turn to the medium run: how wages and prices adjust over time, and how that feeds back into output. The labor market sits at the center of that story.

7.1A tour of the labor market

Definitions
Labor force L
Employed N + unemployed U. Everyone else of working age is out of the labor force.
Unemployment rate
\(u = U/L\): unemployed divided by the labor force.
Participation rate
L / working-age population.
Employment rate
N / working-age population.
Discouraged workers
Not actively looking for a job, but would take one. Counted as out of the labor force, not as unemployed.

Fig 7.1 (US, 2018, millions): non-institutional civilian population 257.7 → labor force 162.0 (employed 155.7, unemployed 6.3) + out of the labor force 95.7. Unemployment rate = 6.3 / 162.0 = 3.9%.

Exam trap · denominatorsu divides by the labor force (N + U). The participation and employment rates divide by the working-age population.
Flows: the labor market is very active

A given unemployment rate can hide an active market (many separations and hires, people move in and out of unemployment fast) or a sclerotic one (few flows, a stagnant pool of long-term unemployed). Separations = quits + layoffs. The Current Population Survey (≈ 60,000 households a month) shows the US flows (Fig 7.2, monthly averages 1996–2018):

  • The flows in and out of unemployment are large relative to the stock, so the average duration of unemployment is about 2 months.
  • Large flows in and out of the labor force go directly to and from employment. Many "out of the labor force" are discouraged workers, which is why economists also watch the employment rate.

  1. How many people are in the labor force?
  2. What is the participation (activity) rate?

Solution
a

\(L = N + U = 1.65 + 0.14\) L = 1.79 million. Out of the labor force: 2.8 − 1.79 = 1.01 million.

b

\(1.79 / 2.8\) participation = 63.9%

c

\(u = 0.14 / 1.79\) u = 7.8%. Employment rate \(= 1.65 / 2.8\) 58.9%.

Reason: discouraged workers want a job but have stopped searching, so they count as out of the labor force, not as unemployed. In a long slump many give up, and u can even fall while the jobless problem gets worse. (Also: part-time workers who want full-time jobs count as employed.)

7.2Movements in unemployment

Since 1948 the US unemployment rate has moved between about 3% and 10%, rising sharply in recessions (Fig 7.3).

  • Fig 7.4: when u is high, the proportion of unemployed finding a job within a month is lower.
  • Fig 7.5: when u is high, a higher proportion of employed workers lose their job.
Key ideaHigh unemployment hurts workers twice: the employed are more likely to lose their job, and the unemployed are less likely to find one, so they stay unemployed longer. This is why u drives workers' bargaining power in 7.3.

7.3Wage determination

  • Collective bargaining (unions and firms) sets slightly more than 10% of US wages, but plays a big role in Japan and most of Europe. The more skilled the job, the more likely wages are bargained individually.
  • Workers are usually paid more than their reservation wage, the wage at which they would be indifferent between working and being unemployed.
  • Bargaining power depends on how costly it is for the firm to replace a worker, and how hard it is for the worker to find another job. Low u means strong bargaining power, so higher wages.
  • Efficiency wages: firms pay above the reservation wage to cut turnover and raise productivity, morale and commitment. When u is low, quitting is easy, so firms raise wages to keep workers. Henry Ford (1914) doubled pay to $5 a day: turnover fell from 370% (1913) to 16% (1915). Amazon did something similar in 2018.

The aggregate nominal wage:

\[ W = P^e\,F(\underset{(-)}{u},\underset{(+)}{z}) \quad\text{(7.1)} \]
  • \(P^e\): workers and firms care about the real wage. Wages are set before the price level is known, so they use the expected price level. \(P^e\) doubles → W doubles.
  • \(u\)↑ → W↓: weaker bargaining power, or firms can pay less and still keep workers willing to work.
  • \(z\) is a catch-all for everything else that raises wages at given \(P^e\) and u: unemployment insurance (benefits), employment protection (costlier layoffs), minimum wages, union strength.
TUT 7 · EX 3

What moves the nominal wage

Using the wage-setting relation, how do these events affect the nominal wage?
  1. A decrease in the expected price level
  2. A decrease in the unemployment rate
  3. A decrease in unemployment benefits
Solution

Start from \(W = P^e F(u, z)\).

a

W ↓ in proportion. Workers expect cheaper goods, so the same real wage needs a lower nominal wage.

b

W ↑. Workers have more bargaining power (easy to find another job), and firms pay more to stop quits.

c

W ↓. z falls: being unemployed is more painful, so workers accept a lower wage at any u.

7.4Price determination

Prices depend on costs, which depend on the production function. Take the simplest one, labor only, with productivity A = 1:

\[ Y = AN \;\xrightarrow{A=1}\; Y = N \quad\text{(7.2)} \qquad\qquad P = (1+m)\,W \quad\text{(7.3)} \]
  • One more unit of output needs one more worker, so the marginal cost is W.
  • Firms are not perfectly competitive: they set the price as a markup m over cost. With perfect competition, m = 0 and P = W.
TUT 7 · EX 4

What moves price setting

Briefly explain how these events affect price setting:
  1. More mergers among firms reduce competition
  2. Stronger antitrust legislation increases competition
  3. A decrease in nominal wages
Solution

Start from \(P = (1+m)W\).

a

Market power rises, so m ↑ → P ↑ at the same W. The real wage \(W/P = 1/(1+m)\) falls.

b

Market power falls, so m ↓ → P ↓ at the same W. The real wage rises.

c

Marginal cost falls, so P ↓ in proportion. m is unchanged, so W/P is unchanged.

7.5The natural rate of unemployment

Assume \(P^e = P\) (expectations are right, as in the medium run). Then wage setting and price setting each give a real wage:

\[ \text{WS: } \frac{W}{P} = F(\underset{(-)}{u},\underset{(+)}{z}) \quad\text{(7.4)} \qquad\qquad \text{PS: } \frac{P}{W} = 1+m \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{W}{P} = \frac{1}{1+m} \quad\text{(7.5–7.6)} \]
  • WS slopes down: the higher u, the lower the real wage chosen by wage setters.
  • PS is horizontal: the real wage implied by price setting depends only on m, not on u (productivity is constant).

Equilibrium: the real wage chosen in wage setting equals the real wage implied by price setting:

\[ F(u_n, z) = \frac{1}{1+m} \quad\text{(7.7)} \]

\(u_n\), the natural (structural) rate of unemployment, depends only on z and m.

TUT 7 · EX 2

The WS-PS model

  1. Show analytically what determines the nominal wage: the wage-setting relation (WS).
  2. Show analytically what determines prices: the price-setting relation (PS).
  3. Assume P = Pᵉ. Combine the two equations, draw the model and write the labor-market equilibrium condition. Why does WS slope down while PS is horizontal? Which two variables are determined where they cross?
Solution
a

\(W = P^e F(u, z)\): the nominal wage rises with the expected price level, falls with u, and rises with z (benefits, employment protection, minimum wage).

b

\(P = (1+m)W\): with Y = N, the marginal cost is W, and firms add the markup m.

c

With \(P^e = P\): WS \(W/P = F(u,z)\), PS \(W/P = 1/(1+m)\). Equilibrium: \(F(u_n, z) = 1/(1+m)\). The diagram is Fig 7.6 above.

WS slopes down: higher u weakens workers' bargaining power and lets firms pay less without losing workers, so the real wage chosen in bargaining falls.

PS is flat: firms charge a fixed markup over W whatever u is, so the real wage they imply, 1/(1+m), does not depend on u.

The intersection determines the real wage W/P and the natural rate un.

TUT 7 · EX 8

Reading the WS relation

Use the wage-setting relation:
  1. What effect does a decrease in u have on the real wage?
  2. Why does WS have a negative slope?
  3. What does an increase in unemployment benefits do to the WS curve?
  4. What does an increase in the rate of structural change do to the WS curve?
Solution
a

W/P ↑: a movement up along WS. Workers can find jobs easily, so they bargain for more.

b

Higher u means weaker bargaining power (it is harder to find another job and easier for the firm to replace you). Firms can also pay a lower efficiency wage when quitting is risky. So the real wage falls as u rises.

c

z↑: unemployment is less painful, so workers ask for more at every u. WS shifts up and un rises (Fig 7.7).

d

More structural change means more jobs destroyed and created, with workers moving between them. At a given u, the unemployed are mostly people between jobs who find new ones quickly, so unemployment is less of a threat and workers bargain harder. Treat it as z↑: WS shifts up, un rises.

TUT 7 · EX 10

Natural rate in numbers

The markup is 5% over costs; wage setting is W = P(1 − u).
  1. What real wage does the wage-setting equation imply?
  2. What is the natural rate of unemployment?
  3. The markup rises to 10%. What happens to the natural rate? Explain.
Solution

Divide by P: W/P = 1 − u. Price setting gives \(W/P = 1/1.05 = 0.952\), which is the real wage in equilibrium.

TUT 7 · EX 5

A higher minimum wage

Show the effect of an increase in the minimum wage in the WS-PS model. Give one economic argument for the effect on the natural rate.
Solution

A higher minimum wage raises z: WS shifts up. PS is unchanged, so W/P stays at 1/(1+m) and un ↑ (A → A′).

Argument: the minimum wage raises the wage workers get at any u, but firms still price at a markup, so the real wage cannot rise. Something must bring wage demands back down: higher unemployment. In concrete terms, firms hire fewer low-productivity workers whose output is worth less than the new minimum.

TUT 7 · EX 6

Lower unemployment benefits

Show the effect of a decrease in unemployment benefits in the WS-PS model. Give one economic argument for the effect on the natural rate.
Solution

Lower benefits lower z: WS shifts down, W/P unchanged, un ↓.

Argument: with lower benefits, losing your job costs more, so workers accept lower wages at any u and the unemployed search harder and take offers sooner. Wage demands fit the PS real wage at a lower u.

TUT 7 · EX 7

More competition, lower markups

Solution

m↓ raises 1/(1+m): PS shifts up. Along WS: W/P ↑, un ↓.

Why W/P rises: with a lower markup, firms charge less over the same wage, so prices fall relative to wages. A higher real wage is consistent with wage setting only at lower unemployment, so un falls. This is one explanation for the falling US natural rate (8.4).

7.6Where we go from here · Appendix

  • So far \(P = P^e\). In the short run P can differ from \(P^e\), so u can differ from un and Y from Yn.
  • Expectations are unlikely to be wrong systematically, so in the medium run output tends to return to its natural level. Chapter 8 drops the assumption \(P = P^e\).
Appendix: WS-PS versus labor supply and demand

Put employment N on the horizontal axis. Since \(U = L - N\) and \(u = 1 - N/L\):

\[ \frac{W}{P} = F\!\left(1 - \frac{N}{L},\, z\right) \]
  • WS now slopes up: higher employment means a higher real wage (it looks like labor supply).
  • PS is still flat at 1/(1+m) (it looks like a flat labor demand).
  • Equilibrium A gives natural employment Nn. The gap between Nn and L is unemployment.
TUT 7 · EX 11

Solution

Substitute \(u = 1 - N/L\) into WS: \(W/P = F(1 - N/L, z)\), increasing in N. PS: \(W/P = 1/(1+m)\). Equilibrium: \(F(1 - N_n/L, z) = 1/(1+m)\).

A lower minimum wage lowers z: WS shifts down / right. With PS unchanged: Nn ↑, unemployment \(L - N_n\) ↓, W/P unchanged.

Chapter eight

The Phillips Curve, the Natural Rate of Unemployment, and Inflation

In 1958 A.W. Phillips found a negative relation between inflation and unemployment in the UK. In 1960 Samuelson and Solow found the same for the US and called it the Phillips curve. US data for 1900–1960 (Fig 8.1) show the pattern: low unemployment came with high inflation, high unemployment with low or negative inflation.

8.0Warm-up: potential output and the business cycle

Definitions
Potential output Yn
Natural level of output: produced when u = un. With Y = N: \(Y_n = L(1-u_n)\).
Output gap
\(Y - Y_n\). Positive: the economy is overheating. Negative: it is in a recession.
Business cycle
Output moves around potential: expansion (trough → peak), contraction (peak → trough).

8.1Inflation, expected inflation, and unemployment

Combine wage setting \(W = P^e F(u,z)\) and price setting \(P = (1+m)W\), now without assuming \(P = P^e\). With \(F(u,z) = 1 - \alpha u + z\):

\[ P = P^e(1+m)(1 - \alpha u + z) \quad\text{(8.1)} \]

In rates of change (appendix: divide by \(P_{t-1}\), write \(P_t/P_{t-1} = 1+\pi_t\), and approximate):

\[ \pi_t = \pi_t^e + (m+z) - \alpha u_t \quad\text{(8.2–8.3)} \]
  • \(\pi^e\)↑ → π↑: wage setters ask for higher W, firms pass it into P.
  • m↑ or z↑ → π↑ (at given πᵉ).
  • u↓ → π↑: tighter labor market, higher wages, higher prices.
TUT 8 · EX 2

The actual price level when P ≠ Pᵉ

Solution

Substitute WS into PS: \(P = (1+m)\,P^e F(u,z)\), and with \(F = 1 - \alpha u + z\): P = Pᵉ(1+m)(1 − αu + z).

In rates: πt = πet + (m + z) − αut

  • πᵉ (+): higher expected inflation → higher nominal wage demands → higher prices, one for one.
  • m (+): a higher markup raises prices for any given wage.
  • z (+): benefits, protection and the like raise wages at any u, which firms pass into prices.
  • u (−): higher unemployment weakens bargaining → lower wages → lower prices. α measures how strongly.

8.2The Phillips curve and its mutations

Original Phillips curve: anchored expectations

If inflation has hovered around a fixed value π̄ for years, people simply expect it: \(\pi_t^e = \bar\pi\). Then (taking π̄ ≈ 0 as in the 1960s):

\[ \pi_t = \bar\pi + (m+z) - \alpha u_t \quad\xrightarrow{\bar\pi = 0}\quad \pi_t = (m+z) - \alpha u_t \quad\text{(8.4)} \]

A stable, negative relation between the level of inflation and u. It fit the US in the 1960s (Fig 8.2: u fell steadily, inflation rose steadily), and policymakers thought they could pick a point on the curve.

De-anchoring: the curve vanishes in the 1970s

From 1970 the relation disappeared (Fig 8.3) because wage setters changed how they formed expectations. Inflation was persistently positive and higher, so they started to use last year's inflation:

\[ \pi_t^e = (1-\theta)\,\bar\pi + \theta\,\pi_{t-1} \quad\text{(8.5)} \]
θPhillips curveu affects…
0\(\pi_t = \bar\pi + (m+z) - \alpha u_t\)the level of π (original)
0 < θ < 1\(\pi_t = [(1-\theta)\bar\pi + (m+z)] + \theta\pi_{t-1} - \alpha u_t\)π, with persistence

US 1970–1995 (Fig 8.4): \(\pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = 7.4\% - 1.2\,u_t\) (8.7), the accelerationist (modified) Phillips curve: low unemployment makes inflation keep rising.

Re-anchoring since the mid-1990s

By the mid-1990s the Fed kept inflation near 2%, and expectations re-anchored. US 1996–2018 (Fig 8.5): \(\pi_t = 2.8\% - 0.16\,u_t\) (8.8). The curve is back to a level relation, but much flatter.

8.3The Phillips curve and the natural rate of unemployment

The natural rate is the u at which inflation equals expected inflation. Set \(\pi = \pi^e\) in (8.3):

\[ u_n = \frac{m+z}{\alpha} \quad\text{(8.9)} \qquad\qquad \pi_t - \pi_t^e = -\alpha\,(u_t - u_n) \quad\text{(8.10)} \]
  • u < un → π > πᵉ. With θ = 1: inflation rises.
  • u > un → π < πᵉ. With θ = 1: inflation falls.
  • un is the NAIRU: the non-accelerating-inflation rate of unemployment.
  • Example with (8.8): \(2\% = 2.8\% - 0.16u_n\) → \(u_n = 0.8/0.16 = 5\%\).
Friedman and Phelps (late 1960s)Theory ahead of the facts: the trade-off is an illusion in the long run. It is temporary, not permanent. Unemployment can stay below un only while inflation keeps surprising people, which needs rising inflation. The 1970s proved them right.

8.4A summary and many warnings

Today's US relation is well captured by the change in inflation and the gap u − un: above un inflation tends to fall, below it inflation tends to rise. Three warnings:

1 · The natural rate differs across countries and over time
  • Europe: labor-market rigidities are blamed: generous unemployment insurance, high employment protection, minimum wages, bargaining rules such as extension agreements. But European unemployment was not always high, and some countries (Denmark, the Netherlands, Ireland) had low rates before the crisis.
  • US since 1990, un fell from 6–7% to about 4%: globalization and competition (lower m), temporary-help agencies, an ageing population, higher incarceration, more workers on disability, and the long-term effects of 2008–09.
2 · High inflation brings wage indexation

Wage indexation automatically raises wages with inflation. If a share λ of contracts is indexed to current inflation:

\[ \pi_t = [\lambda\pi_t + (1-\lambda)\pi_{t-1}] - \alpha(u_t - u_n) \quad\text{(8.11)} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = -\frac{\alpha}{1-\lambda}(u_t - u_n) \]

λ = 0 gives back (8.10). λ > 0 strengthens the effect of unemployment on inflation: lower u raises wages, which raise prices, which immediately raise indexed wages again.

3 · Deflation: the curve breaks down at low inflation

Workers resist nominal wage cuts. In Portugal in 1984 (27% inflation) wage changes were spread roughly symmetrically. In 2012 (2.1% inflation) they bunched at zero with almost no cuts (Fig 8.6). So high unemployment stops pushing inflation down once wage growth reaches zero.

From the Short to the Medium Run: The IS-LM-PC Model

Chapters 3–6 gave short-run output from the goods and financial markets. Chapters 7–8 gave the labor market. Now put them together: IS-LM on top, the Phillips curve below, both with output on the horizontal axis.

9.1The IS-LM-PC model

\[ \text{IS: } Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y,\, r+x) + G \quad\text{(9.1)} \qquad\qquad \text{LM: } r = \bar r \] \[ \text{PC: } \pi - \pi^e = -\alpha(u - u_n) \quad\text{(9.2)} \]

Translate u into Y. With Y = N: \(Y = L(1-u)\), so potential output is \(Y_n = L(1-u_n)\) and the output gap is

\[ Y - Y_n = -L(u - u_n) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \pi - \pi^e = \frac{\alpha}{L}(Y - Y_n) \quad\text{(9.3)} \]
  • Anchored expectations (the tutorial's default): \(\pi^e = \bar\pi\), the central bank's target, so the bottom panel plots \(\pi - \bar\pi\).
  • Backward-looking (the slides): \(\pi^e = \pi_{-1}\), so \(\pi - \pi_{-1} = (\alpha/L)(Y - Y_n)\) (9.4) and the bottom panel plots the change in inflation. Same picture, different label.
  • Either way: Y > Yn → inflation rises above target (or above last year); Y < Yn → it falls.
Okun's law (Focus)Output growth lowers unemployment: \(u - u_{-1} \approx -g_Y\). The US fit (1960–2018) is \(u - u_{-1} = -0.4(g_Y - 3\%)\). Output must grow about 3% a year just to keep u constant, and each point of growth above that lowers u by only 0.4 points (the Okun coefficient), because firms hoard labor and discouraged workers come back into the labor force.

9.2From the short to the medium run

Start at A with a low policy rate: Y > Yn, so inflation keeps rising. To stabilize inflation, the central bank raises r until output is back at Yn (A′, Fig 9.2).

  • The rate at which Y = Yn is rn: the natural, neutral, or Wicksellian rate of interest. It is set by IS: \(Y_n = C(Y_n - T) + I(Y_n, r_n + x) + G\).
  • If the bank wants constant inflation, an initial boom must be followed by a slowdown. If the boom raised inflation and the bank wants it back down to the old level, it must even push output below Yn for a while (point C).
  • In the medium run, output, unemployment and the real rate do not depend on monetary policy. Monetary policy determines only inflation and the nominal rate (\(i = r_n + \bar\pi\)). This is the neutrality of money.

9.3Complications and how things can go wrong

Fig 9.2 makes the adjustment look smooth. Reality is harder:

  1. The central bank does not know Yn exactly, so it does not know how big the output gap is.
  2. The economy takes time to respond to rate changes.
  3. The zero lower bound: i cannot go below 0, so \(r = i - \pi^e \ge -\pi^e\).
The deflation spiral (Fig 9.3)

If output is far below Yn and \(r_n\) is lower than the bank can reach at i = 0, output stays below potential. Inflation falls and turns into deflation. With i stuck at 0, deflation raises the real rate, which lowers output further, which deepens deflation: a deflation spiral (trap). This happened in the Great Depression, and the Great Recession raised the same fear.

Badly timed policy can even amplify the cycle. So "optimal" policy smooths cycles, but it cannot eliminate them.

9.4Fiscal consolidation revisited

Cutting the deficit (G↓ or T↑) shifts IS left. In the short run, at an unchanged r, output falls (A → A′). In the medium run the central bank cuts r to the new, lower r′n, and output returns to Yn (A″). Same output, but a lower interest rate, so more investment (Fig 9.4).

TUT 9 · EX 3

Fiscal consolidation

  1. The government cuts its budget deficit to reduce public debt, starting from Y = Yn. Show the short-run effects. What happens to consumption and investment?
  2. How does the economy return to medium-run equilibrium? What happens to each curve?
Solution
a

G↓ or T↑ shifts IS left. If the bank keeps r, output falls to Y′ < Yn (A′): a recession, and π falls below π̄. C falls (lower income; if taxes rose, lower disposable income too). I falls: sales are lower and r has not changed.

b

Below-target inflation leads the bank to cut r to r′n: LM shifts down, investment recovers and output rises along IS′ back to Yn. On the PC the economy moves back up to π̄. In the new medium run: Y = Yn, r lower, I higher than before. The smaller deficit (G↓) is replaced by more investment. If T rose, C is lower too.

9.5The effects of an increase in the price of oil

Two big real oil-price increases happened (Fig 9.5): the 1970s (OPEC acting as a monopoly) and the 2000s (fast growth of emerging economies). Prices dropped in 2008 (the recession) and after 2014 (US shale, a weaker OPEC).

  • Oil is a non-labor input, so a higher oil price works like a higher markup: PS shifts down, the real wage falls, and un rises (Fig 9.6), so Yn falls.
  • Short run: at the old output, the economy is now above the new Y′n, so inflation rises. The PC shifts left/up.
  • Medium run (if permanent): the bank raises r to r′n, and output falls to Y′n. On the way: stagflation, falling output and high inflation at the same time (Fig 9.7).
Why the 2000s were milder than the 1970sA 100% permanent oil-price rise used to raise the CPI by about 5% and cut GDP by about 6–8%. On post-1987 data the effects are about 3% and 3–4%. Plausible reasons: weaker worker bargaining power (less wage push), and better-anchored inflation expectations thanks to monetary policy.
TUT 9 · EX 5

Supply shock: oil

  1. Show the effects of an oil-price increase, starting at the natural level of output.
  2. Explain what happens in the short run and in the medium run.
Solution
a

Higher oil prices → m↑ → PS down → un↑ → Yn↓: the PC shifts left (it now crosses zero at Y′n). IS is unchanged.

b

Short run: r and output are unchanged at first, but output is now above the new potential, so π rises above target (A′). Real wages fall.

Medium run: to stop inflation the bank raises r to r′n: LM up, output falls along IS to Y′n (A″), and inflation returns to target. Result: lower output, higher un, higher r, lower real wage for as long as oil stays expensive. Along the way, output falls while inflation is high: stagflation.

9.6Conclusions

  • Shocks and policies have different effects in the short and the medium run.
  • Disagreements about policy often come down to how fast the economy adjusts.
  • Movements of output around its trend are output fluctuations (business cycles). They come from shocks and their dynamic effects, the propagation mechanism.

Exam kit

Formula sheet & common traps

Labor market

\(L = N + U\), \(u = U/L\)

Participation = L / pop, employment rate = N / pop

Discouraged workers: out of the labor force

WS-PS

WS: \(W = P^e F(u,z)\) → \(W/P = F(u,z)\)

PS: \(P = (1+m)W\) → \(W/P = 1/(1+m)\)

\(F(u_n,z) = 1/(1+m)\)

z↑: WS up, un↑. m↑: PS down, W/P↓, un↑

Phillips curve

\(\pi_t = \pi_t^e + (m+z) - \alpha u_t\)

\(\pi_t^e = (1-\theta)\bar\pi + \theta\pi_{t-1}\)

θ = 0: level. θ = 1: \(\pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = (m+z) - \alpha u_t\)

\(u_n = (m+z)/\alpha\), \(\pi - \pi^e = -\alpha(u - u_n)\)

Indexation

\(\pi_t - \pi_{t-1} = -\dfrac{\alpha}{1-\lambda}(u_t - u_n)\)

un unchanged, slope steeper

Numeric recipe: divide (stuff − αu) by (1 − λ)

IS-LM-PC

IS: \(Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y, r+x) + G\), LM: \(r = \bar r\)

\(Y_n = L(1-u_n)\), \(Y - Y_n = -L(u - u_n)\)

\(\pi - \pi^e = (\alpha/L)(Y - Y_n)\)

Medium run: Y = Yn, r = rn, π = π̄, \(i = r_n + \bar\pi\)

Okun & ZLB

\(u - u_{-1} = -0.4(g_Y - 3\%)\)

ZLB: \(r = i - \pi^e \ge -\pi^e\)

Deflation at i = 0 → r↑ → Y↓ → more deflation

Ten traps that cost marks
  1. u = U / labor force, not U / population or U / employed.
  2. Discouraged workers are out of the labor force, so they lower u.
  3. WS uses the expected price level Pᵉ, PS the actual P.
  4. A WS shift changes only un. The real wage moves only with m.
  5. Higher markup = PS down (1/(1+m) falls), and oil works like a higher markup.
  6. Anchored πᵉ: low u gives a higher but constant π. θ = 1: π rises every year.
  7. In θ = 1 problems, work year by year: πt = πt−1 + (m + z − αu).
  8. Indexation leaves un unchanged; it only steepens the curve.
  9. In IS-LM-PC the central bank sets the real rate r; i = r + πᵉ.
  10. In the medium run monetary policy cannot change Y, u or r (neutrality). Supply shocks (m, z, oil) do change Yn.
