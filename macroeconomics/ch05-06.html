Goods and Financial Markets: The IS-LM Model

5.1The goods market and the IS relation

Chapter 3 treated investment as constant. Now it depends on sales (production Y) and the interest rate i. Goods-market equilibrium becomes the IS relation (framework by Hicks and Hansen):

\[ I = I(\underset{(+)}{Y},\underset{(-)}{i}) \quad\text{(5.1)} \qquad\qquad Y = C(Y - T) + I(Y, i) + G \quad\text{(5.2)} \]
  • ZZ (demand vs output) slopes up: at a given i, higher Y raises C and I. It is flatter than 45° because demand rises less than one-for-one. It need not be a straight line. Equilibrium: ZZ meets the 45° line.
  • Why IS slopes down: i↑ → I↓ → demand↓ at every Y (ZZ shifts down) → Y↓, amplified by the multiplier. A change in i is a movement along IS.
  • Shifts of IS: anything that changes demand at a given i. G↑, T↓, confidence↑ shift it right; G↓, T↑ shift it left.
Exam trap · along vs shiftThe variable that changed is on an axis (i or Y) → movement along the curve. It is not on an axis (G, T, x…) → the whole curve shifts.

TUT 5 · EX 1

Deriving the IS curve

Why does the IS curve slope down? Show the derivation graphically. How do higher G and higher T affect it?
Solution

Slope: i↑ → I↓ → ZZ shifts down → equilibrium Y falls (left panel). Plot each (Y, i) pair (right panel): that line is IS.

Shifts: G↑ raises demand at every i, so IS shifts right by multiplier × ΔG. T↑ lowers disposable income, so IS shifts left (Fig 5.3) by c₁ × multiplier × ΔT.

5.2Financial markets and the LM relation

Take Chapter 4’s \(M = \$Y\,L(i)\) and divide by the price level P. Real money supply equals real money demand:

\[ \frac{M}{P} = Y\,L(i) \quad\text{(5.3)} \]

The central bank chooses the interest rate and adjusts the money supply to hit it. So the LM relation is simply \(i = \bar i\): a horizontal line. Any point on LM is financial-market equilibrium.

TUT 5 · EX 2

Deriving the LM curve

Convert the money market from nominal to real variables. Why is LM horizontal? Show the derivation graphically.
Solution

Real form: \(M = \$Y L(i)\) with \(\$Y = PY\), divided by P, gives \(M/P = Y L(i)\).

Why horizontal: if Y rises, money demand shifts right, which would push i up. The central bank defends \(\bar i\) by raising M just enough. So i is the same at every Y, and it changes only by the bank’s decision (it is exogenous).

5.3Putting IS and LM together

\[ \text{IS: } Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y, i) + G \qquad\qquad \text{LM: } i = \bar i \]

Only at the intersection are both markets in equilibrium (Fig 5.5).

Vocabulary
Fiscal contraction
G − T falls. Also called fiscal consolidation.
Fiscal expansion
G − T rises.
Monetary expansion
i falls, M rises.
Monetary contraction
i rises, M falls. Also called monetary tightening.

TUT 5 · EX 4

Fiscal contraction

Public debt is 120% of GDP, twice the Maastricht level, and the country is under the Excessive Deficit Procedure. A new government wants to cut borrowing. Using IS-LM, show the effect of lower G on Y and i. What happens to IS, LM, C and I, and why?
Solution

IS shifts left, LM stays. Y ↓, i unchanged. C falls because lower Y lowers disposable income. I falls because sales fall, while i is unchanged. The deficit shrinks, at the cost of lower output.

TUT 5 · EX 6

A tax cut

Using IS-LM, show the effect of lower taxes on Y and i. What happens to IS, LM, C and I, and why?
Solution

IS shifts right, LM stays. Y ↑, i unchanged. C rises twice: first from the tax cut itself, then from higher Y. I rises because sales rise, at an unchanged i.

TUT 5 · EX 5

Raising rates to fight inflation

Inflation is 3.5% against a 2% target, the economy is overheating, and the central bank raises rates. Using IS-LM, show the effect on Y and i. What happens to IS, LM, C and I, and why?
Solution

LM shifts up, IS does not shift (a movement along it). Y ↓, i ↑. I falls for two reasons: higher i and lower sales. C falls because lower Y lowers disposable income. Weaker demand is what cools the overheating economy.

5.3Solving IS-LM with numbers

Method: (1) substitute C, I, G into \(Y = C + I + G\) and solve for Y: that is IS. (2) Set money demand = supply: that is LM. (3) Solve the two together. (4) Find C and I, and check \(C + I + G = Y\).

Exam trap · these two exercises have a fixed MEx 3 and Ex 12 give a fixed money supply, so LM slopes up and i responds to fiscal policy. That is different from the flat LM above, where the bank fixes i. Read which one the question gives you.

5.4Using a policy mix

A monetary-fiscal policy mix combines the two. In a recession, both can raise output: fiscal expansion shifts IS right and monetary expansion shifts LM down (Fig 5.8). A fiscal consolidation with a monetary expansion cuts the deficit without a recession (Fig 5.9).

TUT 5 · EX 7

Cutting the deficit without a recession

A country cuts G to reduce its deficit, which would lower output. What can the central bank do? Show the combination in IS-LM. What happens to C and I? To taxes? So to the deficit?
Solution

The bank lowers i: IS shifts left, LM shifts down, and Y is unchanged.

  • C unchanged: Y and T are unchanged, so YD is too.
  • I rises, by exactly the cut in G, because i is lower and sales are the same.
  • Taxes unchanged, G lower, so the deficit falls.

With Ex 9 numbers: G − 100 needs i to fall from 8% to 2.44%; Y stays 1280, C stays 796, I goes from 84 to 184. Output shifts from public spending to investment.

Focus · The U.S. recession of 2001The recession (March–December 2001) was triggered by a sharp fall in investment demand. The response was strong: the Fed cut the federal funds rate from 6.5% in January to 2% at year end (LM down); President Bush cut taxes in the 2001 and 2002 budgets, and after September 11 spending on defense and homeland security rose (IS right).
Focus · Deficit reduction: good or bad for investment?Since \(I = S + (T - G)\), a lower deficit seems to raise I at given S. But a fiscal contraction lowers output, so S falls by more than \(T - G\) rises, and I falls. Ex 9 numbers, G − 100 at i = 8: T − G rises by 100, S falls by 150 (84 → −66), so I falls by 50 (84 → 34). With the mix of Ex 7, I rises instead.

5.5Does IS-LM fit the facts?

Output adjusts with a lag, so dynamics must be added. Consumers take time to adjust to disposable income; firms take time to adjust investment to sales and to the interest rate, and production to sales.

Evidence (Fig 5.10): after a 1-point rise in the federal funds rate, retail sales, output and employment fall gradually over several quarters (output by about 0.6% at its lowest), and unemployment rises by about 0.1 point. The price level barely moves. So the short-run predictions fit: higher i means lower output, slowly, with prices nearly fixed.

Financial Markets II: The Extended IS-LM Model

6.1Nominal versus real interest rates

So far: two assets, one rate. Now the financial system matters. The nominal rate i is in terms of dollars; the real rate r is in terms of goods.

Derivation (Fig 6.1): sell 1 good for Pt dollars → lend it to get \((1+i_t)P_t\) dollars next year → that buys \((1+i_t)P_t/P^e_{t+1}\) goods. So:

\[ 1 + r_t = (1 + i_t)\frac{P_t}{P^e_{t+1}} \quad\text{(6.1)} \qquad \pi^e_{t+1} = \frac{P^e_{t+1} - P_t}{P_t} \quad\text{(6.2)} \] \[ 1 + r_t = \frac{1 + i_t}{1 + \pi^e_{t+1}} \quad\text{(6.3)} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r_t \approx i_t - \pi^e_{t+1} \quad\text{(6.4)} \]
  • πᵉ = 0 → r = i. Usually πᵉ > 0, so r < i. Higher πᵉ at a given i means lower r.
  • Ex-ante real rate \(i - \pi^e\) (before the fact); ex-post \(i - \pi\) (realized).
  • The rate in the IS relation is the real rate.
  • Since i ≥ 0 (zero lower bound), r cannot go below −π.
  • Fig 6.2: since the early 1980s the U.S. nominal rate has fallen a lot, but expected inflation fell too, so the real rate fell much less.

Two reasons r can rise

From \(r = i - \pi^e\): (1) the nominal rate rises at given πᵉ; (2) expected inflation falls at given i.

TUT 6 · EX 4

i down, r up?

Can the nominal rate fall while the real rate rises over the same period?
Solution

Yes, if expected inflation falls by more than i. Example: i 5% → 4%, πᵉ 3% → 1%, so r goes 2% → 3%.

6.2Risk and risk premia

Some bonds may default, so holders demand a risk premium x. With riskless rate i and default probability p (and nothing paid on default), equal expected returns require:

\[ 1 + i = (1 - p)(1 + i + x) + p\cdot 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{(1 + i)\,p}{1 - p} \]

Example: i = 3%, p = 2% gives x ≈ 2.1%. Two determinants of x: the probability of default and the degree of risk aversion of bond holders.

Fig 6.3: yields on 10-year Treasuries, AAA and BBB corporate bonds. In September 2008 the crisis sharply raised the rates at which firms could borrow.

6.3The role of financial intermediaries

Direct finance: ultimate borrowers borrow from ultimate lenders. Much lending instead goes through financial intermediaries, which receive funds from investors and lend them on.

Bank balance sheet (Fig 6.4)

  • Capital ratio = capital/assets = 20/100 = 20%. Leverage ratio = assets/capital = 100/20 = 5.
  • Higher leverage → higher expected profit rate, and higher risk of insolvency and bankruptcy.
  • Illiquid assets are hard to sell, raising the risk of fire sales (prices far below true value) and insolvency.
  • Liquid liabilities (demand deposits) raise that risk further and can cause bank runs.
Focus · Bank runsRuns fill U.S. history up to the 1930s (see It’s a Wonderful Life). Solutions: narrow banking (banks hold only liquid, safe government bonds, no loans); federal deposit insurance, introduced in 1934; and liquidity provision by the Fed, so that banks can borrow overnight.

6.4Extending the IS-LM model

Add two distinctions: nominal vs real rate, and the policy rate vs the rate borrowers pay.

\[ \text{IS: } Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y,\, i - \pi^e + x) + G \qquad \text{LM: } i = \bar i \]

Since πᵉ is given, choosing i means the central bank effectively chooses the real policy rate r:

\[ \text{IS: } Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y,\, r + x) + G \quad\text{(6.5)} \qquad \text{LM: } r = \bar r \quad\text{(6.6)} \]

Firms and consumers borrow at r + x, the central bank sets only r.

6.5From a housing problem to a financial crisis

Housing (Fig 6.7): prices rose from 2000 to 2006, then fell sharply. Low interest rates in the 2000s stimulated demand, and lenders made loans to risky borrowers: subprime mortgages. From 2006 many mortgages went underwater (mortgage value above house value); borrowers defaulted and lenders took large losses.

Why a housing loss became a financial crisis
High leverage
Banks underestimated risk; managers had incentives to seek high returns without fully counting bankruptcy risk; banks avoided regulation through structured investment vehicles (SIVs).
Securitization
Securities built on a bundle of assets, such as mortgage-based securities (MBS). Senior securities have first claim; junior ones, such as collateralized debt obligations (CDOs), come after. It diversifies risk, but the selling bank had little incentive to keep risk low, and the risk was hard for rating agencies to assess, even for toxic assets.
Wholesale funding
Banks borrow from other banks or investors to buy assets. SIVs were funded entirely this way, which made liabilities liquid, hence run-prone.

To the real economy: demand fell because of the high cost of borrowing, lower stock prices and lower confidence (Fig 6.8: consumer and business confidence bottomed in early 2009). IS shifted left.

The policy response
Financial
Deposit insurance raised from $100,000 to $250,000; widespread Fed liquidity facilities and more assets accepted as collateral; the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP).
Monetary
Federal funds rate at zero by December 2008; unconventional monetary policy: buying other assets to affect borrowers’ rates directly.
Fiscal
American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (February 2009): $780 billion in tax cuts and spending increases.

Formula sheet & common traps

IS

\(Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y,i) + G\)

Slopes down: i↑ → I↓ → Y↓

Right: G↑, T↓. Left: G↓, T↑.

Shift = multiplier × ΔG, or c₁ × multiplier × ΔT

LM

\(M/P = Y\,L(i)\)

\(i = \bar i\): flat, bank adjusts M

M fixed: LM slopes up (Ex 3, 12)

i↑ = LM up; i↓ = LM down

Numeric recipe

1. Y = C + I + G → IS

2. Md = Ms → LM

3. Solve together → Y, i

4. C, I, and check C + I + G = Y

Multiplier when I rises with Y: \(\dfrac{1}{1 - c_1 - b_1}\), with b₁ the coefficient of Y in I

Real rates & risk

\(1 + r = \dfrac{1+i}{1+\pi^e}\), \(r \approx i - \pi^e\)

\(x = \dfrac{(1+i)p}{1-p}\)

Borrowing rate: \(r + x\)

Capital ratio = capital/assets; leverage = assets/capital

Extended IS-LM

\(Y = C(Y-T) + I(Y, r+x) + G\)

\(r = \bar r\)

x↑: IS left. Limit on cutting r: \(r \ge -\pi\)

Ten traps that cost marks
  1. Variable on an axis → movement along; not on an axis → shift.
  2. Fiscal policy shifts IS only; monetary policy shifts LM only.
  3. With flat LM, fiscal policy leaves i unchanged. With fixed M (Ex 3, 12), i moves.
  4. T↓ raises C directly and through Y.
  5. Monetary contraction lowers I twice: higher i and lower sales.
  6. A fiscal contraction lowers I, despite \(I = S + (T - G)\): S falls by more.
  7. Policy mix (G↓, i↓): Y and C are unchanged, I rises, and T is unchanged.
  8. IS uses the real rate plus x; money demand uses the nominal rate.
  9. i can fall while r rises, if πᵉ falls by more.
  10. The central bank sets r, not r + x. Deflation at i = 0 makes r positive.
