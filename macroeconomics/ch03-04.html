3.0The demand–production–income loop

When economists study year-to-year movements in economic activity, they look at how three things interact:

  • changes in the demand for goods lead to changes in production;
  • changes in production lead to changes in income;
  • changes in income lead to changes in the demand for goods.

Chapter 3 turns this circle into a model. In the short run, prices are taken as given and firms produce whatever is demanded, so demand determines output. The whole chapter answers one question: what level of output is consistent with the demand it generates?

3.1The composition of GDP

Before modelling demand, split GDP into the groups of people who buy it.

Key terms
Consumption (C)
Goods and services bought by consumers, from food to cars to haircuts. By far the largest component.
Investment (I)
Also called fixed investment: nonresidential (firms buy new plants and machines) plus residential (people buy new houses or apartments).
Government spending (G)
Purchases of goods and services by federal, state and local governments. It excludes government transfers (pensions, unemployment benefits) because those are not purchases.
Exports (X)
Purchases of domestic goods and services by foreigners.
Imports (IM)
Purchases of foreign goods and services by domestic consumers, firms and government.
Net exports
The trade balance, X − IM. Exports > imports ⇔ trade surplus; imports > exports ⇔ trade deficit.
Inventory investment
Production minus sales. Positive when firms produce more than they sell and the unsold goods are added to inventories.

Put together:

\[ Y = C + I + G + (X - IM) + \text{inventory investment} \]

3.2The demand for goods

Total demand for goods, Z, is defined as

\[ Z \equiv C + I + G + X - IM \]

The sign ≡ marks an identity: true by definition. To keep things simple, the chapter assumes that all firms produce the same good, that firms are willing to supply any amount at a given price, and that the economy is closed (X = IM = 0):

\[ Z \equiv C + I + G \]
Consumption (C)

Consumption depends mainly on disposable income YD, the income left after consumers have received transfers and paid taxes:

\[ C = C(\underset{(+)}{Y_D}) \tag{3.1} \]

C(YD) is the consumption function. It is a behavioural equation because it describes how consumers behave. Assume it is linear, with two parameters:

\[ C = c_0 + c_1 Y_D \tag{3.2} \]
The two parameters
c1
The (marginal) propensity to consume, MPC. Of each extra unit of disposable income, consumers spend c1. It is the slope of the consumption function, with 0 < c1 < 1.
c0
What people would consume if disposable income were zero: the intercept, or autonomous consumption. A change in c0 means consumption changes at a given income, for example because confidence collapses. A lower c0 shifts the whole line down (Figure 3.1).

Disposable income is income minus net taxes, where T = taxes paid minus government transfers received:

\[ Y_D \equiv Y - T \quad\Rightarrow\quad C = c_0 + c_1 (Y - T) \tag{3.3} \]
The saving function (previewing 3.4)

What households do not consume, they save. Private saving is S ≡ YD − C, so with the linear consumption function:

\[ S = Y_D - (c_0 + c_1 Y_D) = -c_0 + (1 - c_1)\,Y_D \]

1 − c1 is the propensity to save (MPS), so MPC + MPS = 1. At YD = 0 saving equals −c0, which is dissaving. Saving is exactly zero at the break-even income YD = c0/(1 − c1), where the consumption line crosses the 45° line.

Exam trapThe MPC is the slope ΔC/ΔYD, not the ratio C/YD. The ratio is the average propensity to consume. Because c0 > 0, the average propensity falls as income rises, while the MPC stays constant.

\( C = 200 + 0.5\cdot 0 = 200 \). C = 200

This is autonomous consumption c0. With no disposable income, people still need to eat and pay rent, so they dissave: they draw down accumulated savings (wealth), sell assets, or borrow.

Because T is fixed, ΔYD = ΔY = +100 and ΔC = +50.

The intercept is c0 = 400: autonomous consumption, what households consume even with zero disposable income. The slope is c1 = 0.75: each extra unit of disposable income raises consumption by 0.75, and the other 0.25 is saved.

\( Y_D = 2000 - 400 = 1600,\qquad C = 400 + 0.75(1600) = 400 + 1200 = 1600 \) C = 1600

At this income C = YD, so saving is zero. Point A lies on the 45° line because 1600 is exactly the break-even income \(400/0.25\).

Households now save 400 out of the same income instead of 0.

Endogenous vs exogenous variables
Key terms
Endogenous
Explained within the model; depends on other variables (here C, Y, Z).
Exogenous
Not explained by the model, taken as given (here I, G, T).

Investment is treated as exogenous for now; the bar marks this:

\[ I = \bar I \tag{3.4} \]

T and G describe fiscal policy, the government’s choice of taxes and spending. They are exogenous because governments do not behave with the regularity of consumers or firms, so the book treats them as variables the government chooses rather than explaining them.

3.3The determination of equilibrium output

Substitute (3.3) and (3.4) into the closed-economy demand identity:

\[ Z = c_0 + c_1(Y - T) + \bar I + G \tag{3.5} \]

Equilibrium in the goods market requires production to equal demand:

\[ Y = Z \tag{3.6} \]

This is an equilibrium condition: it only holds when firms produce exactly what is demanded and inventories do not change unexpectedly. Combining the two:

\[ Y = c_0 + c_1(Y - T) + \bar I + G \tag{3.7} \]

In words: production (Y) equals demand, which depends on income (Y), which is itself equal to production. Y appears on both sides, which is the loop in algebraic form. Macroeconomists always use three tools: algebra to make sure the logic is correct, graphs to build intuition, and words to explain the results.

Tool 1 · Algebra
\[ \begin{aligned} Y &= c_0 + c_1 Y - c_1 T + \bar I + G \\ (1 - c_1)\,Y &= c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1 T \\ Y &= \underbrace{\frac{1}{1 - c_1}}_{\text{multiplier}}\;\underbrace{\big[c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1 T\big]}_{\text{autonomous spending}} \end{aligned} \tag{3.8} \]
Reading equation 3.8
Autonomous spending
\([c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1T]\): the part of demand that does not depend on output. It is positive: with a balanced budget (T = G) and 0 < c1 < 1, \(G - c_1T = (1-c_1)G > 0\).
Multiplier
\(1/(1-c_1)\), which is larger the closer c1 is to 1. With c1 = 0.6 the multiplier is 1/0.4 = 2.5, so a $1 billion rise in c0 raises output by $2.5 billion.
Tool 2 · The graph (Keynesian cross)
  1. Plot production as a function of income. Production equals income, so this is the 45° line (slope 1).
  2. Plot demand as a function of income, the ZZ line: \[ Z = (c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1T) + c_1 Y \tag{3.9} \] Its intercept is autonomous spending and its slope is c1 < 1, so ZZ is flatter than the 45° line.
  3. In equilibrium production equals demand: the economy sits where ZZ crosses the 45° line (point A in Blanchard Fig. 3.2).

Away from the intersection, inventories do the signalling. If demand exceeds production, firms sell from stock (inventories fall unexpectedly) and raise output. If production exceeds demand, unsold goods pile up and firms cut output. This adjustment pushes the economy toward the equilibrium.

TUT 3 · EX 6

Reading the Keynesian cross

Use the graph below.
  1. What does the distance OA represent? What would have to happen for it to decrease?
  2. What do the distances OY1, Y1E and Y1F represent? Is the economy in equilibrium when output equals Y1?
Solution
a

OA = autonomous spending \(c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1T\): the vertical intercept of ZZ, the demand that exists even when income is zero.

OA falls (ZZ shifts down in parallel) if c0 falls (lower consumer confidence), if I falls (lower business confidence), if G falls, or if T rises. Strictly, a higher c1 also lowers OA when T > 0, because the term −c1T grows, but it mainly changes the slope.

b

OY1: the level of output (production = income) measured on the horizontal axis.

Y1F: production at Y1, read off the 45° line. It equals OY1, because along the 45° line the vertical distance equals the horizontal one.

Y1E: demand at income Y1, the height of ZZ.

Not an equilibrium. Y1F > Y1E, so production exceeds demand by EF, an unplanned increase in inventories. Firms cut production and output falls toward the intersection. (At Y2 it is the reverse: demand Y2C exceeds production Y2B, inventories fall by BC, and output rises.)

3.3The multiplier at work

Suppose c0 rises by $1 billion. ZZ shifts up by $1 billion in parallel, and the equilibrium moves from A to A′. Output rises by more than $1 billion. Blanchard’s Figure 3.3 traces the rounds:

  • AB: first-round increase in production. Demand is up by $1bn, so firms produce $1bn more.
  • BC: first-round increase in income. Producing more means paying out $1bn more income.
  • CD: second-round increase in demand. Consumers spend c1 × $1bn of that new income.
  • DE: second-round increase in production and income, and so on.

After n + 1 rounds the total increase in production is a geometric series:

\[ 1 + c_1 + c_1^2 + \dots + c_1^{\,n} = \frac{1 - c_1^{\,n+1}}{1 - c_1} \;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\; \frac{1}{1 - c_1} \]

Tool 3 · Words. Production depends on demand, which depends on income, which is itself equal to production. An increase in demand raises production and income, which in turn raises demand further. The final increase in output is larger than the initial shift in demand, by a factor equal to the multiplier. The multiplier depends on the propensity to consume, which can be estimated with econometrics, the set of statistical methods used in economics.

Dynamics of adjustment. The movement of output over time is called the dynamics of adjustment. How long it takes depends on how and when firms revise their production schedules. If they react quickly to inventory changes, adjustment is fast; if they revise only every quarter, it takes several quarters.

3.3Comparative statics: what moves what

Every shock in this model does one of two things. It shifts ZZ by changing autonomous spending (c0, I, G, T), or it rotates ZZ by changing the slope c1, which also changes the multiplier.

Focus · Lehman, fears of another Great Depression, and shifts in the consumption function

When people start worrying about the future, they save more even if their current income has not changed, so c0 falls. After Lehman Brothers went bankrupt in September 2008, Google searches for “Great Depression” spiked to about three times their average in October 2008. U.S. disposable income barely moved through 2008, yet consumption fell, and consumption of durable goods dropped roughly 11% by 2008 Q4. This is a textbook downward shift of the consumption function: lower C at a given YD.

3.4Investment equals saving: another way to see equilibrium

John Maynard Keynes, in the General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), described the same equilibrium in terms of investment and saving.

Key terms
Private saving (S)
\(S \equiv Y_D - C = Y - T - C\)
Public saving
\(T - G\). If T − G > 0: budget surplus. If T − G < 0: budget deficit.

Start from the equilibrium condition, subtract T from both sides and move C to the left:

\[ \begin{aligned} Y &= C + I + G \\ Y - T - C &= I + G - T \\ S &= I + G - T \\ I &= S + (T - G) \end{aligned} \tag{3.10} \]

This is the IS relation: Investment equals Saving, private plus public. So there are two equivalent ways to state goods-market equilibrium:

Production = Demand  ⇔  Investment = Saving

The behavioural saving function again:

\[ S = Y - T - c_0 - c_1(Y - T) = -c_0 + (1 - c_1)(Y - T) \tag{3.11} \]

with 1 − c1, the propensity to save, between 0 and 1. Insert (3.11) into (3.10):

\[ \bar I = -c_0 + (1-c_1)(Y-T) + (T-G) \;\Longrightarrow\; Y = \frac{1}{1-c_1}\big[c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1T\big] \tag{3.12} \]

This is exactly (3.8). Same model, different lens.

Saving and the paradox of thrift

Z = C + I + G, C = 300 + 0.9YD, T = 1000, I = 200, G = 2000.
  1. Calculate the equilibrium level of income.
  2. Calculate consumption at that level of output.
  3. Write the saving function and calculate saving at equilibrium.
  4. Households raise autonomous saving by 100 and cut autonomous consumption by 100. Find the new equilibrium output. Was the desire to save good or bad for economic activity?
  5. Calculate saving at the new equilibrium and compare it with (c). What happened to saving?
Solution
a

\[ Z = 300 + 0.9(Y - 1000) + 200 + 2000 = 1600 + 0.9Y \;\Rightarrow\; 0.1Y = 1600 \;\Rightarrow\; Y = 16{,}000 \]

Y* = 16,000 (multiplier = 1/0.1 = 10)

b

\( Y_D = 15{,}000,\quad C = 300 + 0.9(15{,}000) = 13{,}800 \) C = 13,800. Check: \(13{,}800 + 200 + 2000 = 16{,}000\) ✓

c

\( S = -300 + 0.1(Y - 1000) \). At Y = 16,000: \(S = -300 + 0.1(15{,}000) = 1200\). S = 1200

IS check: public saving is \(T - G = -1000\) (a deficit), and \(S + (T - G) = 1200 - 1000 = 200 = I\) ✓

d

The intercept of saving rises from −300 to −200, i.e. c0: 300 → 200.

\[ Z' = 1500 + 0.9Y \;\Rightarrow\; Y' = 1500/0.1 = 15{,}000,\qquad \Delta Y = 10 \times (-100) = -1000 \]

Y′ = 15,000. The desire to save had a negative effect: lower consumption means lower demand, lower production and lower income, amplified by the large multiplier of 10.

e

\( S' = -200 + 0.1(15{,}000 - 1000) = -200 + 1400 = 1200 \) S′ = 1200, unchanged

Households tried to save 100 more, but income fell by exactly enough (1000 × MPS 0.1 = 100 less saving) to cancel the attempt. Saving cannot change because in equilibrium \(S = I + G - T = 200 + 2000 - 1000 = 1200\), and I, G and T are fixed. This is the paradox of saving.

Focus · The paradox of saving

We grow up hearing about the virtues of thrift, but this model tells a different story. If consumers decide to save more, c0 falls and (3.12) says output falls. Saving does not even rise: by (3.10), \(I = S + (T - G)\), and since I, T and G are unchanged by assumption, S cannot change. The attempt to save more leads to lower output and unchanged saving. This is a short-run result. In the medium and long run, higher saving can finance more investment and raise output (later chapters).

3.5Is the government omnipotent? A warning

Equation (3.8) suggests the government can choose G or T to hit any level of output it wants. Reality is harder, and the model leaves out a lot:

  • Changing G or T is not easy; budgets take time to pass.
  • Investment and imports may respond too, which makes the effects of policy hard to assess (Chapters 5, 9, 18–20).
  • Expectations are likely to matter (Chapters 14–16).
  • The output effects may be unsustainable in the medium run (Chapter 9).
  • Cutting T or raising G can lead to large deficits and public debt in the long run (Chapters 9, 11, 16, 22).

Automatic (built-in) stabilizers

So far G and T did not depend on income. In reality tax revenues rise with income. Consider C = c0 + c1YD, T = t0 + t1Y, YD = Y − T, with G and I constant and 0 < t1 < 1.
  1. Calculate equilibrium output.
  2. What is the multiplier? Does the economy respond more strongly to changes in autonomous spending when t1 is zero or when it is positive?
  3. Why is fiscal policy in this case called an automatic stabilizer?
Solution
a

\[ \begin{aligned} Y &= c_0 + c_1\big(Y - t_0 - t_1Y\big) + I + G \\ Y - c_1(1 - t_1)\,Y &= c_0 - c_1t_0 + I + G \\ Y &= \frac{1}{1 - c_1(1 - t_1)}\,\big[c_0 - c_1t_0 + I + G\big] \end{aligned} \]

b

\[ \text{multiplier} = \frac{1}{1 - c_1(1-t_1)} = \frac{1}{1 - c_1 + c_1t_1} \]

With t1 > 0 the denominator is larger, so the multiplier is smaller than \(1/(1-c_1)\). The economy responds more strongly when t1 = 0. Each extra unit of income raises disposable income by only 1 − t1, so every round of re-spending is smaller. The slope of ZZ is c1(1 − t1) instead of c1.

With Ex 4 numbers (c1 = 0.5) and t1 = 0.2, the multiplier is \(1/(1 - 0.4) \approx 1.67\) instead of 2. With c1 = 0.8 and t1 = 0.25 it is \(1/(1-0.6) = 2.5\) instead of 5. Try the “Ex 9” preset in the lab above.

c

Nobody has to pass a law. When autonomous spending falls and output starts to drop, tax payments fall automatically (and transfers such as unemployment benefits rise), cushioning disposable income and consumption.   1. Demand: \(Z \equiv C + I + G\) in a closed economy, with \(C = c_0 + c_1(Y-T)\), I and G exogenous.
  2. Equilibrium: \(Y = Z\) gives \(Y = \frac{1}{1-c_1}[c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1T]\), multiplier × autonomous spending.

  5. The multiplier is the limit of the rounds \(1 + c_1 + c_1^2 + \dots\)
  6. Equivalent condition: \(I = S + (T - G)\), the IS relation. Wanting to save more lowers Y but leaves S unchanged (paradox of saving).

Financial Markets I

4.1 Demand for money4.2 Interest rate I4.3 Interest rate II4.4 Liquidity trap

Financial markets look intimidating, but they play an essential role in the economy. This chapter focuses on one question: how is the interest rate on bonds determined, and what role does the central bank play? In the U.S. the central bank is the Federal Reserve Bank, or the Fed. The chapter builds the answer in two steps: first an economy without banks (4.2), then one with banks (4.3).

4.1The demand for money

Simplify the financial world to two assets:

Two assets
Money
Can be used for transactions but pays no interest. Two kinds: currency (coins and bills) and checkable deposits (bank deposits you can write checks on or use a card against).
Bonds
Pay a positive interest rate i but cannot be used for transactions.

How you split your wealth between them depends on two things: your level of transactions (more spending needs more money) and the interest rate on bonds (the reward you give up by holding money). You can also hold bonds indirectly through money market funds (money market mutual funds). In the early 1980s their interest rate reached 14% a year, so people moved a lot of wealth out of checking accounts and into these funds.

The money demand function

Aggregate demand for money Md equals nominal income times a decreasing function of the interest rate:

\[ M^d = \$Y \, L(\underset{(-)}{i}) \tag{4.1} \]
  • Proportional to nominal income \(\$Y\), which measures the level of transactions. If prices or real output double, people need twice as much money.
  • Decreasing in i: the interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money. A higher i makes people move wealth into bonds and hold less money.

For a given \(\$Y\), plotting \(M^d\) against i gives the downward-sloping Md curve.

Exam trap · shift or move along?A change in i is a movement along Md. A change in $Y (real income or the price level) shifts Md. Wealth only decides how much goes into bonds; it does not enter Md.

4.2Determining the interest rate I: no banks

Assume for now that there are no banks: all money is currency, issued by the central bank. The central bank decides to supply an amount M:

\[ M^s = M \]

Equilibrium in financial markets requires that supply equals demand, \(M^s = M^d\):

\[ M = \$Y\,L(i) \tag{4.2} \]

Read (4.2) as: the interest rate must be such that people are willing to hold exactly the amount of money the central bank has supplied. The supply curve is vertical because M is chosen by the central bank and does not depend on i (Figure 4.2).

How the market gets there. If i were above equilibrium, people would want to hold less money than exists. They would try to buy bonds, which raises bond prices and lowers i until they are content to hold M. Below equilibrium the reverse happens.

Open market operations

Central banks change the money supply by buying or selling bonds in the bond market: open market operations.

  • Expansionary: the central bank buys bonds and pays with newly created money. M rises and i falls.
  • Contractionary: the central bank sells bonds and takes the money it receives out of circulation. M falls and i rises.

The central bank’s assets are the bonds it holds; its liabilities are the money it has issued (Figure 4.5). An open market purchase raises both sides by the same amount.

4.2Bonds, prices and interest rates

Take a one-year bond such as a U.S. Treasury bill (T-bill) that promises to pay $100 a year from now. If its price today is \(\$P_B\), the interest rate on the bond is the return from buying it now and collecting $100 later:

\[ i = \frac{\$100 - \$P_B}{\$P_B} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \$P_B = \frac{\$100}{1 + i} \]
  • The higher the price of the bond, the lower the interest rate.
  • The higher the interest rate, the lower the price today.

This is why an open market purchase lowers i: the central bank’s extra demand for bonds pushes up \(P_B\), which by the formula means a lower i. More generally, with face value F, \(i = (F - P_B)/P_B\).

Instead of choosing M and letting i adjust, the central bank can choose the interest rate and then adjust the money supply to achieve it. \[ H = H^d \tag{4.5} \] \[ H = \theta\,\$Y\,L(i) \tag{4.6} \]

An increase in H lowers the interest rate, and a decrease in H raises it (Figure 4.7). Dividing (4.6) by θ gives \(H/\theta = \$Y L(i)\): the model of 4.2 again, with money supply \(M = H/\theta\). Extension: people hold both currency and deposits

If a fraction c of money is held as currency, \(CU = cM\), deposits are \(D = (1-c)M\), reserves are \(R = \theta D\), and

\[ H = CU + R = \big[c + \theta(1-c)\big]\,M \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = \frac{1}{c + \theta(1 - c)}\,H \]

With c = 0 the multiplier is 1/θ; with c = 1 (no banks) it is 1. That is section 4.2.

The federal funds market

In practice, the market for central bank money is the federal funds market: a market where banks with spare reserves lend them overnight to banks that need them. Focus · Will bitcoins replace dollars?

Bitcoins are virtual assets that can be used for transactions; in December 2018 the total value in circulation was about $67 billion. They are unlikely to replace dollars for three reasons: (1) most prices are quoted in dollars, so paying in bitcoin carries price risk; (2) verifying bitcoin transactions takes too much electricity; (3) governments do not want bitcoin to become the accepted currency, because they want to keep control of monetary policy.

4.4The liquidity trap

The interest rate cannot go below zero: the zero lower bound. If bonds paid a negative rate, people would simply hold money, which pays zero.

Once i reaches zero and people hold enough money for their transactions, they become indifferent between money and bonds: both pay nothing. Beyond that point the money demand curve is horizontal at i = 0 (Figure 4.8). Further increases in the money supply then have no effect on the interest rate; people simply hold the extra money. The economy is in a liquidity trap: monetary policy cannot lower the interest rate any further.

TUT 4 · EX 9

A severe recession and continuous monetary expansion

The economy falls into a severe recession and the central bank keeps increasing the money supply to support it. What happens to the interest rate after some time? What is this situation called, and what is the constraint on the interest rate called? Is monetary policy very effective against a recession if the economy is already in this situation?
Solution

Each expansion lowers i, until the interest rate reaches zero. It cannot fall further: that constraint is the zero lower bound. The situation is a liquidity trap: money demand is horizontal at i = 0, so additional money is simply held (by households as deposits, by banks as reserves) and the interest rate stays at zero.

So no, conventional monetary policy is not effective once the economy is in a liquidity trap. The central bank can still increase M, but it can no longer lower i to stimulate demand. Other tools, such as fiscal policy, have to carry more of the load.

  5. Off equilibrium, the signal is unplanned inventory change, not prices.
  6. Paradox of saving: Y falls, S is unchanged (not “S rises”).
  7. i changes → move along Md; $Y changes → shift Md.
  8. A shift of Md with vertical Ms changes i but not the quantity of money.
  9. Bond price and interest rate move in opposite directions.
  10. In a liquidity trap the central bank can still raise M; it just cannot lower i.
Bridge to Chapter 5 · IS–LM

In Chapter 3, investment was exogenous. In Chapter 5 investment depends on the interest rate, so the goods-market equilibrium becomes a relation between Y and i: the IS curve. Chapter 4 showed the central bank effectively chooses i: the LM relation. Put together, they determine output and the interest rate at the same time.
