The Goods Market

3.1The composition of GDP

The loop behind the chapter: demand → production → income → demand. In the short run, prices are given and firms produce whatever is demanded. The question: what output is consistent with the demand it generates?

Key terms
Consumption (C)
Goods and services bought by consumers. By far the largest component.
Investment (I)
Fixed investment: nonresidential (plants, machines) + residential (new houses).
Government (G)
Government purchases of goods and services. Excludes transfers (pensions, benefits), which are not purchases.
Exports (X)
Foreigners’ purchases of domestic goods.
Imports (IM)
Domestic purchases of foreign goods.
Net exports
Trade balance, X − IM: surplus if positive, deficit if negative.
Inventory investment
Production minus sales.
\[ Y = C + I + G + (X - IM) + \text{inventory investment} \]

3.2The demand for goods

Assume all firms produce one good, supply any amount at a given price, and the economy is closed (X = IM = 0). The sign ≡ marks an identity (true by definition):

\[ Z \equiv C + I + G \]

Consumption depends on disposable income, income after taxes and transfers. This consumption function is a behavioural equation:

\[ C = C(\underset{(+)}{Y_D}) \quad\text{(3.1)} \qquad C = c_0 + c_1 Y_D \quad\text{(3.2)} \qquad Y_D \equiv Y - T \quad\text{(3.3)} \]
c1
Propensity to consume (MPC): the slope, between 0 and 1.
c0
Consumption at zero disposable income: the intercept, or autonomous consumption. A fall (e.g. confidence collapses) shifts the whole line down (Fig 3.1).
T
Taxes paid minus transfers received.

Saving: \(S \equiv Y_D - C = -c_0 + (1 - c_1)Y_D\). 1 − c1 is the propensity to save (MPS), so MPC + MPS = 1. At YD = 0, S = −c0 (dissaving). S = 0 at the break-even income \(Y_D = c_0/(1-c_1)\), where C crosses the 45° line.

Exam trapMPC is the slope ΔC/ΔYD, not C/YD (the average propensity, which falls as income rises because c0 > 0).

3.3The determination of equilibrium output

\[ Z = c_0 + c_1(Y - T) + \bar I + G \quad\text{(3.5)} \qquad Y = Z \quad\text{(3.6)} \] \[ Y = c_0 + c_1(Y - T) + \bar I + G \tag{3.7} \]

Y = Z is an equilibrium condition: it holds only when firms produce exactly what is demanded and inventories don’t change unexpectedly. Macroeconomists use three tools: algebra (logic), graphs (intuition), words (explanation).

Tool 1 · Algebra. Solve for Y:

\[ Y = \underbrace{\frac{1}{1 - c_1}}_{\text{multiplier}}\;\underbrace{\big[c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1 T\big]}_{\text{autonomous spending}} \tag{3.8} \]
  • Autonomous spending: demand that doesn’t depend on output. It is positive: with T = G, \(G - c_1T = (1-c_1)G > 0\).
  • Multiplier: larger the closer c1 is to 1. With c1 = 0.6 it is 2.5, so a $1bn rise in c0 raises output by $2.5bn.

Tool 2 · The graph (Keynesian cross). Production = income is the 45° line. Demand is the ZZ line, flatter because c1 < 1. Equilibrium is where they cross (Fig 3.2):

\[ Z = (c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1T) + c_1 Y \tag{3.9} \]

Off equilibrium, inventories signal: demand > production → stocks fall unexpectedly → firms raise output. Production > demand → stocks pile up → firms cut output.

3.3The multiplier at work

c0 rises by $1bn: ZZ shifts up by $1bn and equilibrium moves from A to A′. Rounds (Fig 3.3): AB production +$1bn → BC income +$1bn → CD demand + c1 × $1bn → DE production and income, and so on. A geometric series:

\[ 1 + c_1 + c_1^2 + \dots + c_1^{\,n} = \frac{1 - c_1^{\,n+1}}{1 - c_1} \;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\; \frac{1}{1 - c_1} \]

Tool 3 · Words. Production depends on demand, which depends on income, which equals production. So higher demand raises production and income, which raises demand further. The multiplier depends on the propensity to consume, estimated with econometrics.

Dynamics of adjustment: the movement of output over time. Its speed depends on how often firms revise production: quickly if they react to inventories at once, several quarters if they revise quarterly.

3.3Comparative statics: what moves what

A shock either shifts ZZ (c0, I, G, T change autonomous spending) or rotates it (c1 changes the slope and the multiplier).

Focus · Lehman, fears of another Great Depression, and c0

When people worry about the future, they save more at the same income: c0 falls. After Lehman’s bankruptcy (September 2008), Google searches for “Great Depression” hit about three times their average in October 2008. 3.4Investment equals saving

Keynes (General Theory, 1936) described the same equilibrium through saving. Private saving \(S \equiv Y - T - C\). Public saving \(T - G\): positive = budget surplus, negative = budget deficit. From Y = C + I + G, subtract T and move C:

\[ S = I + G - T \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I = S + (T - G) \tag{3.10} \]

This is the IS relation: Investment = Saving (private + public). So production = demand ⇔ investment = saving. With the saving function:

\[ S = -c_0 + (1 - c_1)(Y - T) \tag{3.11} \] \[ \bar I = -c_0 + (1-c_1)(Y-T) + (T-G) \] \[ \Longrightarrow\; Y = \frac{1}{1-c_1}\big[c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1T\big] \tag{3.12} \]

Exactly (3.8): same model, different lens.

Focus · The paradox of saving

If consumers try to save more, c0 falls and output falls (3.12). Saving doesn’t rise: by (3.10), I, T and G are unchanged, so S cannot change. This is a short-run result; in the medium and long run, higher saving can finance investment and raise output.

3.5Is the government omnipotent? A warning

(3.8) suggests the government can hit any output with G or T. In reality:

  • changing G or T is not easy: budgets take time;
  • investment and imports may respond too (Ch 5, 9, 18–20);
  • expectations matter (Ch 14–16);
  • output effects may be unsustainable in the medium run (Ch 9);
  • lower T or higher G can mean large deficits and debt in the long run (Ch 9, 11, 16, 22).
TUT 3 · APPENDIX 9

Automatic (built-in) stabilizers

Tax revenues rise with income: C = c0 + c1YD, T = t0 + t1Y, G and I constant, 0 < t1 < 1.
  1. Equilibrium output?
  2. The multiplier? Does the economy respond more strongly with t1 zero or positive?
  3. Why is this fiscal policy called an automatic stabilizer?
Solution
a

\[ \begin{aligned} Y &= c_0 + c_1(Y - t_0 - t_1Y) + I + G \\ Y &= \frac{1}{1 - c_1(1 - t_1)}\,\big[c_0 - c_1t_0 + I + G\big] \end{aligned} \]

b

Multiplier \(\frac{1}{1 - c_1(1-t_1)}\) is smaller than \(1/(1-c_1)\), so the response is stronger when t1 = 0. Each extra unit of income raises YD by only 1 − t1; the slope of ZZ is c1(1 − t1). Examples: c1 = 0.5, t1 = 0.2 gives ≈ 1.67 instead of 2; c1 = 0.8, t1 = 0.25 gives 2.5 instead of 5.

c

No law is needed. When output falls, taxes fall automatically (and benefits rise), cushioning YD and C; in a boom, taxes rise and slow demand. The tax system dampens fluctuations by itself.

Financial Markets I

4.1The demand for money

The question: how is the interest rate on bonds determined, and what does the central bank do? (U.S.: the Federal Reserve, the Fed.) First without banks (4.2), then with banks (4.3). Two assets:

Money
Used for transactions, pays no interest: currency (coins, bills) + checkable deposits.
Bonds
Pay interest i, can’t be used for transactions.

The split depends on your level of transactions and the interest rate on bonds. You can also hold bonds through money market funds: in the early 1980s their rate reached 14%, so people moved a lot of wealth out of checking accounts into them.

4.2Determining the interest rate I: no banks

No banks: all money is currency, issued by the central bank. Equilibrium: \(M^s = M^d\), with \(M^s = M\):

\[ M = \$Y\,L(i) \tag{4.2} \]

i must be such that people willingly hold exactly the supplied M. Supply is vertical (Fig 4.2): M doesn’t depend on i. Adjustment: if i is above equilibrium, people want less money than exists, buy bonds, bond prices rise and i falls. Below equilibrium, the reverse.

Draw money supply with money demand. Why is supply vertical? What is determined at the intersection, and what is the equilibrium condition?
Solution

Vertical: the central bank chooses M independently of i (exogenous). The intersection determines the equilibrium i. Condition: \(M^s = M^d\), i.e. \(M = \$Y\,L(i)\). See “Starting point” below.

M↑ lowers i (Fig 4.3): supply shifts right. $Y↑ raises i at given M (Fig 4.4): demand shifts right, people sell bonds, bond prices fall, i rises.

4.2Bonds, prices and interest rates

A one-year Treasury bill (T-bill) pays $100 in a year. With price \(\$P_B\) today (for face value F: \(i = (F - P_B)/P_B\)):

\[ i = \frac{\$100 - \$P_B}{\$P_B} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \$P_B = \frac{\$100}{1 + i} \]

Higher bond price = lower interest rate, and vice versa. That’s why a purchase lowers i: extra demand pushes up \(P_B\).

Fig 4.6: reserves are an asset of banks and a liability of the central bank. With no currency held, money = deposits, and banks hold reserves in proportion:

\[ M^d = \$Y\,L(i) \quad\text{(4.3)} \qquad H^d = \theta M^d \quad\text{(4.4)} \] \[ H = H^d \quad\text{(4.5)} \qquad H = \theta\,\$Y\,L(i) \quad\text{(4.6)} \]

H↑ lowers i, H↓ raises it (Fig 4.7). Dividing by θ gives \(H/\theta = \$Y L(i)\): the 4.2 model with \(M = H/\theta\). Money multiplier = 1/θ: each unit of H supports 1/θ of deposits.

With currency too: a fraction c of money is currency: CU = cM, D = (1 − c)M, R = θD:

\[ H = CU + R = \big[c + \theta(1-c)\big]\,M \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = \frac{1}{c + \theta(1 - c)}\,H \]

c = 0: multiplier 1/θ. c = 1 (no banks): multiplier 1.

Focus · Will bitcoins replace dollars?

Virtual assets usable for transactions; about $67bn in circulation (December 2018). Unlikely, because (1) most prices are in dollars, so paying in bitcoin carries price risk; (2) verifying transactions uses too much electricity; (3) governments want to keep control of monetary policy.

4.4The liquidity trap

i can’t go below zero, the zero lower bound: at a negative rate people would just hold money. Once i = 0 and people have enough money for transactions, they’re indifferent between money and bonds, so Md is horizontal at i = 0 (Fig 4.8). More money then has no effect on i: a liquidity trap.

TUT 4 · EX 9

A severe recession and continuous expansion

In a severe recession the bank keeps raising the money supply. What happens to i after some time? What is this situation called, and the constraint on i? Is monetary policy effective then?
Solution

i falls until it reaches zero: the zero lower bound. The situation is a liquidity trap: extra money is simply held (households’ deposits, banks’ reserves). So conventional policy is not effective: the bank can raise M but can’t lower i. Other tools, such as fiscal policy, must carry more of the load.

Formula sheet & common traps

Goods market

\(Z = c_0 + c_1(Y-T) + \bar I + G\)

\(Y = \dfrac{1}{1-c_1}[c_0 + \bar I + G - c_1T]\)

\(\Delta Y = \dfrac{1}{1-c_1}\,\Delta(\text{autonomous})\)

With \(T = t_0 + t_1Y\): multiplier \(\dfrac{1}{1 - c_1(1-t_1)}\)

Saving

\(S = Y_D - C = -c_0 + (1-c_1)Y_D\)

Break-even \(Y_D = c_0/(1-c_1)\)

\(I = S + (T - G)\) (IS relation)

MPC + MPS = 1, multiplier = 1/MPS

Money market

\(M^d = \$Y\,L(i)\), \(L' < 0\)

No banks: \(M = \$Y L(i)\)

Banks: \(H = \theta\,\$Y L(i)\), \(M = H/\theta\)

With currency: \(M = \dfrac{H}{c + \theta(1-c)}\), \(H = CU + R\)

Bonds

\(i = \dfrac{F - P_B}{P_B}\), \(P_B = \dfrac{F}{1+i}\)

Buy bonds → \(P_B\) ↑ → i ↓ → M ↑

Sell bonds → \(P_B\) ↓ → i ↑ → M ↓

i ≥ 0: zero lower bound

Ten traps that cost marks
  1. Forgetting the −c1T term in autonomous spending (Ex 3: the intercept is 1000, not 1200).
  2. Changing c1 changes the slope and (with T > 0) the intercept of ZZ (Ex 5: 1300 → 1180).
  3. MPC = slope ΔC/ΔYD, not C/YD.
  4. Z ≡ C + I + G is an identity; Y = Z is an equilibrium condition.
  5. Off equilibrium, the signal is unplanned inventory change, not prices.
  6. Paradox of saving: Y falls, S is unchanged (not “S rises”).
  7. i changes → move along Md; $Y changes → shift Md.
  8. A shift of Md with vertical Ms changes i but not the quantity of money.
  9. Bond price and interest rate move in opposite directions.
  10. In a liquidity trap the central bank can still raise M; it just cannot lower i.
Bridge to Chapter 5 · IS–LM

In Chapter 3, investment was exogenous. In Chapter 5 investment depends on the interest rate, so the goods-market equilibrium becomes a relation between Y and i: the IS curve. Chapter 4 showed the central bank effectively chooses i: the LM relation. Put together, they determine output and the interest rate at the same time.
