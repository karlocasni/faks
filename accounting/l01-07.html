Part one · Lecture 1

Foundations

Definitions, users, the set of financial statements and the concepts and principles behind them. Mostly theory points.

1.1What accounting is

Accounting is the continual process of capturing financial data, organizing it and producing financial reports for people who make economic decisions. It is called the “language of business”. The day-to-day record-keeping part is bookkeeping.

Key terms
Business transaction
An economic event that directly changes a company’s financial position or its results.
Stakeholder
Anyone with an interest in the company’s performance. Internal: owners, managers, employees. External: investors, creditors, tax authorities, regulators, customers, unions.
Double-entry
Every transaction is recorded at least twice: a debit in one account and a credit in another. Total debits = total credits, which is how errors are found.
Types of business
Manufacturing (makes products), merchandising (resells goods, like the exam’s company “Villi”), service.
Modern accounting content: 3 types (fill-in every year)
TypeWhat it doesFor whom
Financial accountingRecords transactions and reports them in financial statements (bookkeeping)External users
Cost accountingInternal efficiency: measures costs and revenues by parts of the company (accounting analysis)Internal
Managerial accountingPlanning and control of costs and revenuesManagers

Do not confuse it with traditional accounting content, which has five parts: accounting planning, bookkeeping, accounting control, accounting analysis and providing information.

1.2The set of financial statements

The full set has five statements. The exam’s correct answer is: balance sheet, income statement, cash flow statement, statement of changes in equity and notes to the financial statements. A distractor always slips in “ledger”, “journal” or “depreciation statement”; those are books or schedules, not statements.

StatementShowsTime
Balance sheetFinancial position: assets = liabilities + equityA point in time (static)
Income statement (P&L)Success: revenues − expenses = profit or lossA period (dynamic)
Cash flow statementCash in and out, in 3 activities: operating, investing, financingA period
Statement of changes in equity / retained earningsOwners’ investment, dividends, net income; the “bridge” from income statement to balance sheetA period
NotesExtra information, need not be numbers: accounting policies, uncertainties, statistics—

The six basic accounting categories: assets, liabilities, equity, revenues, expenses, financial result.

Order of preparation in the accounting cycle: income statement → retained earnings statement → balance sheet, because profit flows into equity.

1.3Concepts, principles, standards

The hierarchy is concepts → principles → standards → accounting policies. Each level makes the one above it more concrete.

Concepts (assumptions)
IFRS concepts
Accrual basis and going concern. These two are the official ones.
Accrual basis
Revenues and expenses are recorded when earned / incurred, not when cash is received or paid.
Going concern
The company is expected to keep operating into the foreseeable future.
Monetary unit
Money is the unit of measurement.
Economic entity
Whose asset is it? The company is separate from its owners.
Time period
Life of a business is cut into artificial periods (quarter, year).
IFRS principles

Understandability, cost, relevance, materiality, reliability, true presentation, substance over form, neutrality, prudence, full disclosure, comparability. The ones defined in class:

  • Cost principle: assets are recorded at acquisition cost.
  • Relevance: the information makes a difference to decisions. Reliability: free of error and bias.
  • Comparability: can compare companies. Consistency: same methods year to year.
  • Full disclosure: disclose anything that would change an investor’s or creditor’s view.
Constraints
  • Materiality: if an item makes no difference, the rule need not be followed strictly.
  • Conservatism (prudence): in grey areas choose what does not overstate assets or income.
GAAP covers 3 areas

Valuation (at what value), recognition (asset or expense?), disclosure (what to explain).

Standards and policies

IFRS are international standards (made by the IASB); national standards (in Croatia HSFI / CFRS) cover national specifics. Accounting policies are the choices the standards allow a company (for example depreciation method, inventory method); they must be disclosed in the notes.

The Balance Sheet

Classify every item, then put the classes in the right order. That is the whole of exam task 1 (6 points) and at least one multiple-choice question.

2.1Assets

An asset is a resource the company controls that will bring future economic benefit. The dividing line is one year from the balance sheet date: realised within a year → current; longer → non-current.

GroupTypical items (the exam picks from these)
I · NON-CURRENT (long-term) ASSETS
1. IntangiblePatents, licences, software, concessions, franchise, trademarks, goodwill, advances for intangible assets
2. TangibleLand, buildings, plant, equipment, tools, furniture, motor vehicles, advances for tangible assets
3. FinancialGiven long-term loans, long-term deposits given, bought long-term securities, shares in related companies
4. ReceivablesReceivables due in more than 1 year (sales on credit)
II · CURRENT (short-term) ASSETS
1. InventoriesRaw materials, work in progress, finished goods, merchandise, advances for inventories
2. ReceivablesAccounts receivable (customers), from employees, from the state (VAT receivable), other
3. Financial assetsGiven short-term loans, short-term deposits, bought short-term securities
4. CashCash at bank, cash in hand (register)

2.2Sources of assets: equity and liabilities

Liabilities are obligations to transfer assets later, because of a past event (owed to suppliers, lenders, banks, employees, the state). Equity is the residual: Equity = Assets − Liabilities. In a bankruptcy creditors are paid first; owners get what is left.

GroupTypical items
I · EQUITY
Paid-inOwner’s / shareholders’ equity (share capital, common stock), additional paid-in capital
EarnedReserves (legal, statutory, other), retained earnings, net income of the year (or loss, as a minus)
II · NON-CURRENT LIABILITIES (due after more than 1 year)
Received long-term loans, issued long-term bonds / securities, long-term accounts payable
III · CURRENT LIABILITIES (due within 1 year)
Accounts payable, salaries payable, received short-term loans, issued short-term securities, income tax liability, VAT liability, dividends payable, received advances
Trap: given vs received, bought vs issued
  • Given loan / deposit and bought securities = money we lent out → asset (financial).
  • Received loan and issued bonds = money we borrowed → liability.
  • “Issued bonds on the 3-year period” → more than 1 year → non-current liability.
  • Retained earnings → equity, never a liability.

Equity is divided into paid-in and earned equity (fill-in every year).

2.3Ordering criteria

Liquidity = how fast an asset turns into cash. Cash is the most liquid; intangible assets are the least. The question is just: where does cash stand?

Part three · Lectures 3, 4, 5

The Recording Process

Debit and credit rules, the four balance sheet changes, business books, revenues and expenses, VAT and merchandise. Everything you need for exam task 2 (19 points) and half the theory.

3.1The accounting equation and the T-account

Double-entry bookkeeping is governed by the accounting equation. If revenue equals expenses the basic equation holds:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

During a period revenues and expenses differ, so the extended equation is:

A = L + E + (Revenue − Expenses)

An account is the record of increases and decreases of one item. A T-account has three elements (fill-in): 1. a title, 2. a left (debit) side, 3. a right (credit) side. The balance is the difference of the two sides: bigger debit side → debit balance, bigger credit side → credit balance.

There are five basic account types: assets, liabilities, equity (balance sheet accounts) and revenues, expenses (income statement accounts).

MC trap, asked in 2024 and 2025“Beginning value on T-account expenses / revenues is recorded…” → they don’t have a beginning value. Revenues and expenses are temporary accounts: they start every period at zero and are closed into the result at the end. Only balance sheet (permanent) accounts carry a beginning balance S°, because of the going concern concept.

How revenues and expenses touch the balance sheet:

  • Revenue comes with an increase of an asset (cash, AR) or a decrease of a liability.
  • Expense comes with a decrease of an asset (inventory sold, cash paid) or an increase of a liability (salaries owed, invoice received).

Journal convention: debits are written first, the credit is indented below.

3.3The 4 balance sheet changes

Every balance sheet transaction falls into exactly one of four types. Asked every year in the fill-in: “Changes that cause just a change in qualitative meaning are concentric and perifern balance sheet changes.”

ChangeEffectTotal changes?NatureExample
Centripetal (towards the company)Asset ↑   Source ↑Yes, upQuantitative + qualitativeBuy merchandise on credit; owner invests cash
Centrifugal (out of the company)Asset ↓   Source ↓Yes, downQuantitative + qualitativePay a supplier from the bank account

Note: one slide says centrifugal is “decrease of asset and increase of sources”; that is a typo. Both sides decrease, otherwise the balance sheet would not balance.

3.4Documents, transactions and business books

4 conditions for recording a transaction (fill-in, 4 points)
  1. There is an accounting (bookkeeping) document: written proof of the business event (invoice, sales slip, cheque, bank statement).
  2. It influences assets, sources of assets, revenues or expenses.
  3. It happened in the past.
  4. It is measurable in money.

External transactions happen between the company and an outside party (these are recorded). Internal events happen within the company. Test: is the financial position changed? Buying a computer or paying a bill: yes. Discussing a design with a customer or hiring someone: no.

Business books (fill-in: name 2 main books)
Journal
Book of original entry. Chronological (by date). Shows the whole effect of a transaction in one place and helps find errors.
Ledger
The whole group of accounts. Organised by accounts, not dates, in financial statement order.
Subledgers
Analytical / support books: per customer, supplier, raw material, finished good.
Posting
Transferring journal entries to ledger accounts. Done chronologically and on time.
Trial balance
List of all accounts with their debit and credit balances; proves debits = credits and is the starting point for the statements.
Chart of accounts
Numbering of all accounts. In Croatia each company makes its own; it is not set by law.
Accounting cycle

Analyse → journalize → post to T-accounts → trial balance → adjusting entries → adjusted trial balance → financial statements (IS → RE statement → BS) → close temporary accounts.

3.5Revenues, expenses and the income statement

Definitions (IFRS wording)
Revenue
Increase in economic benefits in the period (inflow of assets or decrease of liabilities) that increases equity, other than owners’ contributions.
Expense
Decrease in economic benefits (outflow of assets or new liabilities) that decreases equity, other than distributions to owners.
Revenue recognition
Recognise revenue in the period it is earned (goods delivered / service done) and collection is reasonably certain.
Matching principle
Expenses go into the same period as the revenues they helped produce.
Accrual basis
Revenues and expenses are recorded when earned / incurred, not when cash is received or paid. (2-point fill-in)
Gains / losses
Difference between what is received and the book value given up (e.g. selling equipment). Shown separately, since selling assets is not the main business.
Retained earnings
All net income since formation minus dividends paid. Not the same as cash.

Adjusting entries (end of period) always touch one income statement account and one balance sheet account, never cash. Two families: prepayments (prepaid expenses, unearned revenues) and accruals (accrued revenues, accrued expenses).

Two methods for preparing the income statement (MC 2025)

The answer is “by nature types method and functional method.” (Direct / indirect is for the cash flow statement; average cost / FIFO is for inventory.)

3.7Merchandise: purchasing cost and cost of goods sold

Merchandise is recorded first at purchasing (acquisition) cost (cost principle) and later at the lower of purchasing cost and net market value (IAS 2).

Purchasing cost (MC every year)

Purchasing price + dependent (depending) costs + customs duties + non-refundable taxes − discounts

Dependent costs = transport, loading, unloading, insurance on the way. VAT receivable is never part of it, because it is refunded by the state. That eliminates every option with “VAT”.

Recording at purchasing cost: the “calculation of supply” route

Each cost component first gets its own account, then all are gathered in Calculation of supply (obračun nabave) and moved into the inventory at once.

Alternative: recording at selling price (lecture 5)

The inventory is kept at selling price = purchasing cost + difference in price (DIP, the margin). On a sale, expenses are recorded at selling price and the DIP of sold goods is then reversed. In retail shops the calculated VAT is also included in the inventory price. The exam task so far always says “recorded by actual purchasing cost”, so the route above is the one to know cold.

3.8From costs to the financial result

Costs such as electricity, salaries, rent, advertising and insurance are first recorded on cost accounts. At period end they move to expenses in two hops, then everything closes into the result:

(8)Cost transference D / each cost account C
→
(8a)Period expenses D / Cost transference C
→
(10)Revenues D / Difference of revenue & expenses C
→
(10a)Difference D / all expense accounts C
→
(10b–c)Gross income → tax + net income
  • Gross income (profit before tax) = revenues − expenses.
  • Income tax = gross income × rate (10% in the papers; Croatia also uses 18%).
  • Net income = gross income − tax. It goes to equity (earned).
  • If expenses > revenues, it is a loss: D Loss / C Difference, and there is no tax.

The student-side tip from your notes holds: revenues are closed from the left (debit) side because they were booked on the right; expenses are closed from the right (credit) side.

Part four · Lectures 6 & 7

Long-Term Assets

Not yet on the past papers’ assignments, but in the lectures and fair game for multiple choice and fill-ins. Learn the definitions, the 4 formulas and the entries.

4.1Long-term intangible assets (LTIA)

Long-term assets are not meant for sale, last longer than one year and transfer their value gradually onto new products. Tax rule: value > 500 EUR and useful life > 1 year.

Intangible assets have no physical form, their life and benefits are hard to predict and they are often hard to sell separately. Types: R&D expenditures, patents, licences, software, concessions, franchise, trademarks, other rights, goodwill (only if bought), advances.

Research
Always an expense of the period: future benefit cannot be proved yet.
Development
Capitalised as an intangible asset if all criteria hold (technical feasibility, intention and ability to use or sell, a market, resources to finish, cost reliably measurable). Otherwise a period expense.
Patent
Exclusive right to use an own innovation after registration. Bought: at purchase price + dependent costs (legal, admin fees).
Licence
A bought right to use someone else’s patented innovation.
Franchise
Paying another company for the right to run its business for a time, purpose and area (fast food).
Concession
Right granted by the state (roads, water, oil, gas) for a fee; recorded as LTIA and amortised over the concession period.
Trademark
Recognised only if bought; internally developed is not an asset.
Goodwill
Reputation. Only bought goodwill (purchase of a whole business) is recorded: cost − fair value of net assets acquired. Not amortised, only written down if impaired.

Valuation: at the start, acquisition cost (price after discounts + dependent costs + customs + excise). Later, either the cost model (cost − amortisation − write-downs) or the revaluation model (fair value). Standards: IAS 38, HSFI/CFRS 5.

4.2Long-term tangible assets (LTTA) and depreciation

Recognised as an asset if future benefits are probable and the cost can be measured reliably. Acquisition cost = purchase price (incl. customs, excise, after discounts) + all costs to bring it to working condition: site preparation, delivery, installation, architects’ and engineers’ fees, estimated dismantling cost. Standards: IAS 16, IAS 36, IAS 40, IFRS 5.

  • Time methods: proportional (straight-line, same every year), degressive (falling), progressive (rising). Plus the functional (units of activity) method.
  • Depreciation starts the month after the asset is put into use. It is cost allocation, not valuation, and is an accounting policy that affects the result.
  • Not depreciated: land, forest, art, archives, assets in preparation, advances.
  • Impairment: if carrying amount > recoverable amount (the higher of fair value / net selling price and value in use).
  • Stocktake (inventory process): actual > book → surplus; actual < book → deficit.
  • Ways an asset leaves: sale, disposal, donation, inventory deficit.

4.3Long-term financial assets and receivables

Long-term financial assets are financial investments for more than one year that bring interest or dividends: shares in related companies, given loans, participating interests, securities, given deposits (IAS 32, IAS 39, IFRS 7). Long-term receivables are due after more than one year: from related companies, from sales on credit, other.

On the short-term side (lecture 5): receivables come from customers (AR, domestic and abroad), employees (advances for trips, extra-paid salary, damages), and the state (mostly VAT receivable). Cash is cash at bank and cash in the register; payments between companies are cashless. A letter of credit is a bank’s promise to pay the seller: a payment-insurance instrument.
