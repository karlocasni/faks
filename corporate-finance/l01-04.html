An Overview of Financial Management

What?Who?Where?To what purpose?Who, revisited (agency)Keys to success

1.1What is corporate finance about?

The lecture is organised around five questions: what corporate finance is, who does it, where (in which form of business), to what purpose, and who again, this time asking whose interests are really being served.

Key terms
Finance
Managing money. More generally, managing the financial side of business activities. Every business transaction begins and ends with a financial transaction, because money is tied up in all assets.
Corporate
All enterprises do business, and the principles apply generally. But they are derived from one particular form, the corporation listed on the stock market, and apply in full only to that most complex form.

1.2The role of the financial manager

The financial manager stands between the firm’s real assets (the business) and the financial markets (a pool of financial assets). Money moves in a loop:

Five primary activities of financial management
  1. Cash management
  2. Minimising the cost of capital
  3. Strategic investment (capital budgeting)
  4. Allocation of income: dividends vs retained earnings
  5. Risk management

As the diagram suggests, these activities fall into two roles of the financial manager: Treasury (raising and managing the money) and Financial planning & analysis (deciding how it is used inside the firm).

  2. External financing: stock sold to outsiders (friends, family, private investors, venture capitalists).
  3. Banks and/or an initial public offering (IPO).
  4. Later: banks, issuing debt, selling additional shares.

1.4The primary objective of the corporation

Profit? Short or long term? Survival? None of these. The primary goal is long-term shareholder wealth maximisation.

Key terms
Market price
The stock price we observe in the financial markets.
Fundamental price
The price that reflects all relevant information. Investors rarely have all of it, so the two can differ.

Shareholder wealth maximisation means maximising the fundamental price of the common stock, not just the current market price.

Economic value: three basic facts
  1. Any financial asset, including a company’s stock, is valuable only to the extent that it generates cash flow.
  2. The timing of cash flow matters: cash received sooner is better.
  3. Investors are risk averse: they pay more for a stock whose cash flows are relatively certain.

So a firm’s value is the present value of the free cash flows it can generate, discounted at the weighted average cost of capital:

\[ \text{Value} = \frac{FCF_1}{(1+WACC)^1} + \frac{FCF_2}{(1+WACC)^2} + \dots + \frac{FCF_\infty}{(1+WACC)^\infty} \]

Managers can raise value in exactly three ways, one for each fact:

  1. Increase the size of the expected cash flows (bigger numerators);
  2. Speed up their receipt (smaller exponents);
  3. Reduce their risk (a lower WACC in the denominator).
Risk–return trade-offThe three levers pull against each other: projects with bigger cash flows are usually riskier. Managers and investors both face this trade-off between risk and reward.

1.5Agency relationships

In proprietorships, partnerships and small corporations, the owners are also the managers. In large corporations they usually are not: shareholders elect directors, who hire managers. What stops managers acting in their own interest rather than the owners’? That question is the agency problem, and the answer is the company’s corporate governance.

Key terms
Agency relationship
Exists whenever a principal hires an agent to act on his or her behalf.
Agency problem
A conflict of interest that arises when the agents entrusted to look after the principals’ interests use their authority for their own benefit instead.

Agency problems exist between:

  • shareholders and managers;
  • shareholders and creditors;
  • majority and minority shareholders;
  • shareholders who are also employees and those who are not; and others.
Shareholders vs managers

Managers are naturally inclined to act in their own interest. Four factors pull their behaviour back toward the owners’:

  1. managerial compensation plans (pay tied to the stock price);
  2. direct intervention by shareholders;
  3. the threat of firing;
  4. the threat of takeover.
Shareholders vs creditors

Shareholders, through managers, could take actions that raise the stock price at the expense of creditors (for example, much riskier projects or much more debt).

In the long run, creditors respond by charging more: the cost of debt rises and ultimately the stock price falls.

1.6What all successful firms do

Two goals
  1. They identify, create and deliver highly valued products or services, which happens only if they offer more value than competitors (lower prices or better products).
  2. They sell at prices high enough to cover costs and compensate owners and creditors for their risk. Profit has to be high enough to compensate investors adequately.
Three key attributes
  1. Skilled people at all levels: leaders, managers and workforce.
  2. Strong relationships with outside groups: win-win relationships with suppliers, and rapidly growing relationships with third-party outsourcers.
  3. Enough capital to execute plans and support operations: land, buildings, equipment, materials. Firms reinvest part of their earnings, but most must also raise funds externally by selling stock and/or borrowing in the financial markets (Lecture 2).
SELF-CHECK 2

Agency in practice

  1. A CEO is paid a fixed salary only, and plans to retire in two years. Which agency problem is likely, and which of the four disciplining factors would you strengthen?
  2. A firm with lots of debt takes on a very risky project. Who gains if it succeeds, who loses if it fails, and what happens to the firm’s cost of debt over time?
Solution
a

Shareholders vs managers. With a fixed salary and a short horizon, the CEO gains nothing from long-term value and may avoid risky but valuable projects, or favour perks. Fix it with the compensation plan: tie pay to the long-term stock price (e.g., shares that vest after several years). Board intervention and the threats of firing or takeover also discipline the CEO.

b

Shareholders vs creditors. If the project succeeds, shareholders keep the upside because creditors only receive fixed interest. If it fails, creditors bear much of the loss, since shareholders’ liability is limited. Creditors anticipate this and raise the cost of debt, which in the long run lowers the stock price.

Lecture 1 recap

  1. Finance is managing the financial side of business; the principles come from the listed corporation.
  2. The financial manager is an intermediator (toward markets) and an internal optimiser (toward the business). Five activities: cash, cost of capital, capital budgeting, dividends vs retention, risk.
  3. Corporation: limited liability, unlimited life, easy transfer and easy capital raising (once public), at the cost of double taxation and filing costs.
  4. Goal: maximise long-term shareholder wealth, meaning the fundamental stock price.
  5. \(\text{Value} = \sum FCF_t/(1+WACC)^t\). Levers: size, timing, risk.
  6. Agency problems (shareholders vs managers, vs creditors, …) are handled by corporate governance: pay, intervention, firing, takeover.

2.6The cost of money: the required rate of return

In a free economy, capital is allocated through a price system. The supply of funds by providers and the demand by users determine the cost (price) of money, the rate users pay to providers.

Debt
The price is the interest rate.
Equity
The price is the cost of equity: the dividends and capital gains that shareholders expect.

The cost to the user is the return to the provider, so cost of capital, required return and rate of return are used interchangeably. The basic equation:

\[ r = r_{RF} + r_R \]

where r is the cost of capital, rRF the (nominal) risk-free rate and rR the risk premium of the particular security. The more risk, the higher the price of money.

2.7Building up the interest rate

Split the risk-free rate into a real part and an inflation part, and the risk premium into its three sources:

\[ r = \underbrace{r_{rf} + r_i}_{r_{RF}} + \underbrace{r_{default} + r_{liquidity} + r_{maturity}}_{r_R} \]
Key terms
rrf · real risk-free rate
The rate on a riskless security if there were no inflation. Typically 1%–4% per year in the USA.
ri · inflation premium
Compensation for expected inflation. All market interest rates include it.
rRF · nominal risk-free rate
\(r_{rf} + r_i\): the rate on (short-term) government securities.
rdefault
Compensation for the risk that the issuer will not pay interest or principal.
rliquidity
Compensation for a security that cannot quickly be sold near its fair value.
rmaturity
Compensation for longer maturity: long bonds lose more value when rates rise.

The real risk-free rate is 2% and expected inflation is 3%. A 10-year corporate bond has a default premium of 1.5%, a liquidity premium of 0.5% and a maturity premium of 1%.
  1. What is the nominal risk-free rate?
  2. What is the bond’s risk premium and its required return?
  3. Expected inflation rises to 4%. What happens to the required return on every security?
Solution
a

\(r_{RF} = 2\% + 3\% = 5\%\) r_RF = 5%

b

\(r_R = 1.5\% + 0.5\% + 1\% = 3\%\), so \(r = 5\% + 3\% = 8\%\). r = 8%

c

The inflation premium is part of \(r_{RF}\), which sits inside every market rate, so all required returns rise by 1 percentage point (this bond: 9%). Risk premiums are unchanged.

2.8Risk structure and term structure of interest rates

Two ways of comparing yields. The risk structure compares issuers of different risk at the same maturity. The term structure compares maturities for the same issuer.

Term structure and the yield curve

Securities of the same issuer, and so the same default risk, can still have different yields because of different maturities. Plotting yield against maturity gives the yield curve. Its traditional (normal) shape is upward-sloping.

Three theories of the yield curve’s shape
Expectations theory
Long rates are averages of today’s and the expected future short rates. If short rates are expected to rise, the curve slopes up; if they are expected to fall, it slopes down.
Liquidity preference theory
Investors prefer short-term securities (more liquid, less price risk), so they demand a premium for long maturities. This explains why the curve normally slopes up even when rates are expected to stay flat. Preferred habitat theory is a variant: investors have a preferred maturity and leave it only for a premium.
Segmented markets theory
Short and long maturities are separate markets, each with its own supply and demand (e.g. banks lend short, pension funds invest long), so each segment sets its own rate.

  1. In the risk-structure figure, why does the BB curve lie above the AAA curve at every maturity?

3.1The annual report

The annual report gives two types of information:

  1. A verbal section that explains why things happened: the year’s operating results and new developments that will affect future operations.
  2. Four basic financial statements that show what happened:
    • the balance sheet,
    • the income statement,
    • the statement of retained earnings,
    • the statement of cash flows.

Together they give an accounting picture of the firm’s operations and financial position. They are audited and mandatory for listed companies, which publish them even quarterly.

3.2The balance sheet

A snapshot of the firm’s financial position at one point in time. The left side shows what is owned; the right side shows how it is financed.

\[ \text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad E = A - L \]
  • Assets are listed in order of liquidity: how long it typically takes to convert them to cash at fair market value.
  • Claims (liabilities and equity) are listed in the order they must be paid: suppliers (accounts payable), banks (notes payable), bondholders… Stockholders come last. Their claim is ownership and need never be “paid off”; it is a residual claim, paid only after all other claimants.
  • Amounts are book values: what bookkeepers recorded when assets were bought or liabilities issued (historical data), not market values (current prices in the market).
Five main components
Assets
Current assetsheld short term, used on a “one-time basis”
Fixed assetsheld long term, “used” gradually over time
Liabilities & equity
Current liabilitiesmaturity up to 1 year
Long-term liabilitiesmaturity over 1 year
Equityowners’ capital; no maturity while the company exists
Long-term liabilities + equity = the company’s capital (long-term financing)
Current assets (most liquid first)
Cash & equivalents
Cash equivalents are marketable securities that mature very soon and convert to cash at close to book value.
Short-term investments
Marketable securities with a longer time to maturity, whose market values are less predictable.
Accounts receivable
Sales for which the company has not yet been paid.
Inventories
Raw materials, work in process and finished goods available for sale.
Fixed assets
Physical assets
Land, buildings, plant, equipment, vehicles. Depreciation applies (estimated wear and tear), except on land.
Long-term investments
Securities held long term, e.g. the stock of a subsidiary.
Intangibles
Patents, copyrights, trademarks and goodwill. Amortization applies.

Depreciation and amortization are annual charges that reflect the estimated cost of the assets used up each year.

Liabilities
Accounts payable
What the company owes its suppliers.
Notes payable
A loan that must be repaid within a year.
Accruals
What the company owes its employees (wages) and the state (taxes).
Long-term liabilities
Bonds, long-term loans.
Equity
Preferred stock
A hybrid of common stock and debt. In bankruptcy it ranks below debt but above common stock. The preferred dividend is fixed, so holders do not benefit when earnings grow. Most firms use little or none, so “equity” usually means common equity.
Common stock
When a company sells shares, the proceeds are recorded in this account.
Retained earnings
The cumulative earnings that have not been paid out as dividends.
Common equity
Common stock + retained earnings, also called net worth: the assets net of liabilities.
A managerial view: working capital
\[ \text{(Gross) working capital} = \text{Current assets} \qquad \text{Net working capital} = \text{Current assets} - \text{Current liabilities} \]

Managers focus on net operating working capital: the operating current assets (cash, receivables, inventories) minus the operating current liabilities that arise from operations (payables, accruals), not interest-bearing loans. The part of current assets the firm needs permanently should be financed with long-term sources.

The income statement summarises revenues and expenses over a period (month, quarter, year) to determine income (profit). Compare: the balance sheet is a snapshot at a point in time; the income statement covers performance during the period.

3.3Volume, fixed costs and profit

The contribution format makes one thing visible: when the number of units changes, sales and variable costs change in proportion, fixed costs and interest do not. So profit moves faster than sales, in both directions. Written as one formula, with q units at price p and variable cost v per unit, fixed costs F and interest I:

\[ \text{Net income} = \big[\,q\,(p - v) - (F + I)\,\big]\,(1 - t) \qquad \text{(if EBT} > 0) \]
Exam trap · losses are not taxedWhen earnings before taxes are negative, the tax is zero and net income equals EBT. Do not multiply a loss by (1 − t). The tutorial key does exactly this: Problem 5 has EBT = −2,000 and NI = −2,000.

3.5Net income ≠ net cash flow

A business’s net cash flow generally differs from its accounting profit, because some revenues and expenses on the income statement were not received or paid in cash during the year. Depreciation and amortization are noncash charges: they reduce net income but nobody is paid, so we add them back:

\[ \text{Net cash flow} = \text{Net income} + \text{Depreciation and amortization} \]

To be precise, net cash flow = net income − noncash revenues + noncash charges. In many cases the other noncash items roughly net out to zero, which gives the simplified formula. There are several cash-flow concepts (net cash flow, operating cash flow, free cash flow), so always check which one is asked.

Why profit and cash differ
  • Profit requires assumptions (how much input was used, at what price). Cash flows involve no assumptions and no ambiguity.
  • But cash flow can be derived from profit, and companies rely on profit to generate cash (their internal source of funding).
  • Cash flows and revenues/expenses can mismatch in timing, in definition and in use.

3.7Depreciation (and interest) as a tax shield

A machine with a 10-year life is bought for 1,000,000. The 1,000,000 is not expensed in the purchase year; it is spread over the depreciable life, 100,000 per year, and deducted from revenues along with labour and materials.

  • Without depreciation, profits would be overstated and taxes too high. Depreciation lowers taxable income: it provides a tax shield.
  • But the 100,000 charged each year is not a cash outlay like labour or materials. It is a noncash charge, so it is added back to net income to get net cash flow.

Analysis of Financial Statements

4.1Why analyse financial statements?

Ratio analysis reveals whether a company’s position has been improving or deteriorating over time, and its strengths and weaknesses compared with other companies in the same industry. It allows you to:

  • analyse the direction and size of changes, and trends;
  • compare the company’s performance (efficiency) with similar companies, the best and the worst company, and the industry average;
  • determine the company’s strategy, or track its implementation (planning).
Three methods: absolute values matter, but so do trends
Trend analysis
Plot a ratio over time to see whether the firm’s condition is improving or deteriorating.
Common-size analysis
Divide every income-statement item by sales and every balance-sheet item by total assets. This makes statements comparable over time and across companies of different size.
Percent-change analysis
Compute growth rates for all income-statement and balance-sheet items.
Three main users
  1. Managers within the firm: to analyse, control and improve operations.
  2. Credit analysts (banks, bond-rating analysts): can the company pay its debts?
  3. Stock analysts: efficiency, risk and growth prospects.

4.2Major limitations of ratio analysis

  • Multidivisional firms: a meaningful industry average is hard to build, so ratios are more useful for small, narrowly focused firms.
  • Inflation distorts the balance sheet (recorded values differ from “true” values) and also depreciation and inventory costs, and so profit. Compare a firm over time, or firms of different ages, with judgement.
  • Seasonal factors: e.g. tourism has different inventory levels in and after the season. Use monthly averages for inventories and receivables in turnover ratios.
  • Window dressing by management (making the statements look better at the reporting date).
  • Different accounting practices: inventory valuation and depreciation methods, leases.
  • Some ratios are hard to judge as good or bad. A high current ratio may mean strong liquidity (good) or excess cash (bad). A high fixed-asset turnover may mean efficient use, or a firm that cannot afford enough assets.
  • A mix of good and bad ratios: what to conclude? Many banks use statistical techniques.
  • Accuracy of data: Enron, WorldCom.
1 · LiquidityCan the firm meet its short-term obligations?
2 · Asset managementIs it using its assets effectively?
3 · Debt managementHow is it financed, and can it service its debt?
4 · ProfitabilityThe combined result of the three groups above.
5 · Market valueWhat investors think of all of it.

4.3Liquidity ratios

A liquid asset trades in an active market and can quickly be converted to cash at the going price. Company liquidity asks: will the company have trouble meeting its obligations?

\[ \text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \qquad \text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventories}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \qquad \text{Cash ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash \& equivalents}}{\text{Current liabilities}} \]
  • The current ratio is the most common measure of short-term solvency: how far short-term creditors’ claims are covered by assets that should turn into cash fairly quickly. It is related to net working capital.
  • The quick (acid-test) ratio removes inventories, the least liquid current assets and the ones on which losses are most likely if the firm has to rely on them to pay short-term obligations.
  • Creditors like a high current ratio. Shareholders may not: it can mean too many nonproductive assets (excess cash, marketable securities) or large inventories that could become obsolete.
Industry averages are not magic numbersNo firm has to hit the average. But a ratio far from it is a red flag: find out why. Say a low current ratio traces to low inventories. Is that a competitive advantage from just-in-time management, or an Achilles’ heel that makes the firm miss shipments and lose sales? Ratios don’t answer this, but they point to where to look.

4.4Asset management ratios

How effectively does the company manage its assets? Is each asset reasonable in view of current and projected sales? Excessive assets lower free cash flow and the stock price. Insufficient assets mean lost sales and lower profitability, free cash flow and stock price. These ratios complement the liquidity ratios: they measure how long cash is tied up in other assets.

\[ \text{Turnover ratio} = \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Asset item}} \qquad\qquad \text{Conversion period} = \frac{365}{\text{Turnover ratio}} = \frac{\text{Asset item}}{\text{Sales}/365} \]

Average asset values should be used where available.

The four ratios
Inventory turnover
\(\dfrac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Inventories}}\). Below the industry average → probably too much inventory: unproductive, earning a low or zero return, possibly obsolete. Inventory conversion period \(= \dfrac{\text{Inventory}}{\text{Sales}/365}\).
Days sales outstanding
\(DSO = \dfrac{\text{Receivables}}{\text{Annual sales}/365}\), the average collection period: how long the firm waits after a sale before receiving cash. Longer is better for customers (and sales), but the firm must finance it. Compare with the industry average and the firm’s own credit terms. Too long → funds are tied up and customers may be in financial trouble.
Fixed assets turnover
\(\dfrac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Net fixed assets}}\): how effectively plant and equipment are used. Fixed assets are at historical cost, so inflation understates old assets and an old firm will tend to show a higher turnover than a new one. That reflects accounting, not efficiency.
Total assets turnover
\(\dfrac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Total assets}}\). Below average → not enough business for the asset base: raise sales, sell assets, or both.

In the exam, use whichever your instructor states, and write the day count down. Also note: in this course inventory turnover = Sales / Inventory (not cost of goods sold).

4.5Debt management ratios

How far is the company financed with debt (financial leverage), how likely is it to default, and how much more debt could it take on?

\[ \text{Debt ratio} = \frac{\text{Total liabilities}}{\text{Total assets}} \]
  • It shows the share of funds provided by current liabilities and long-term debt.
  • Creditors prefer low debt ratios: a bigger cushion against losses in liquidation.
  • Shareholders may prefer more leverage because it magnifies expected earnings. But if returns are leveraged, so are the risks.
\[ \text{Times-interest-earned (TIE)} = \frac{EBIT}{\text{Interest charges}} \]

TIE shows the ability to pay interest: how far operating income can fall before the firm cannot meet its annual interest, or how much more debt it could carry. Two shortcomings: (1) interest is not the only fixed financial charge (principal repayments, lease payments); (2) EBIT is not all the cash flow available to service debt (depreciation is added back in cash flow).

\[ \text{EBITDA coverage} = \frac{EBITDA + \text{Lease payments}}{\text{Interest} + \text{Principal payments} + \text{Lease payments}} \]

EBITDA coverage measures the ability to service debt. It matters most to short-term lenders such as banks: over a short period, depreciation-generated funds can be used to service debt, but over a longer time they must be reinvested to maintain the plant. Bondholders focus on TIE.

Exam trapThe debt ratio uses total liabilities, current liabilities included, not just long-term debt. And debt-to-equity is a different ratio: D/E = TL/E, so D/E = 1 corresponds to a debt ratio of 50%.

4.6Profitability ratios

Profitability shows the combined effect of liquidity, asset management and debt management on operating results, in two ways: profitability of sales (margins) and of assets (or invested capital).

The ratios, as defined in this course
Net profit margin
\(\dfrac{\text{NI available to common}}{\text{Sales}}\). Low margins can come from inefficient operations or from heavy use of debt (high interest), so a low net margin does not always signal an operating problem. “Profit margin” without a qualifier means this one.
Gross profit margin
\(\dfrac{EBIT}{\text{Sales}}\). A low value usually signals operational inefficiency.
Basic earning power
\(BEP = \dfrac{EBIT}{\text{Total assets}}\). Not a margin: the raw earning power of the assets, before taxes and leverage. Useful for comparing firms with different tax situations and degrees of financial leverage.
Return on assets
\(ROA = \dfrac{\text{NI available to common}}{\text{Total assets}}\)
Return on equity
\(ROE = \dfrac{\text{NI available to common}}{\text{Common equity}}\): the most important accounting ratio, the stockholders’ return in an accounting sense.
Exam traps · definitions
  1. In this course, “gross profit margin” = EBIT / Sales. Many textbooks call that the operating margin and use “gross profit” for sales minus cost of goods sold. Use the course definition.
  2. Some books compute ROA as EBIT / total assets. Here that is BEP; ROA uses net income. Always check a ratio’s definition.
  3. Net margin, ROA and ROE use net income minus preferred dividends.

4.7Market value ratios

Market value ratios relate the stock price to earnings, cash flow and book value per share. They show management what investors think of past performance and future prospects. If the other four groups look good, these will be high too.

Key formulas
Earnings per share
\(EPS = \dfrac{\text{NI available to common}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}\)
Dividends per share
\(DPS = d \times EPS\), where d is the dividend payout ratio.
Price/earnings
\(P/E = \dfrac{\text{Price per share}}{EPS}\): how much investors pay per unit of reported profit. High for strong growth prospects, low for riskier firms.
Price/cash flow
\(\dfrac{\text{Price per share}}{\text{Cash flow per share}}\): how much investors pay per unit of cash flow.
Market/book
\(M/B = \dfrac{\text{Market price per share}}{\text{Book value per share}}\), with \(BPS = \dfrac{\text{Common equity}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}\).

Firms with high ROE generally sell at higher multiples of book value. M/B usually exceeds 1: book value records the past (what stockholders have invested), while the market price is forward-looking and includes expected future cash flows.

4.8DuPont analysis

In ratio analysis it is easy to miss the forest for the trees. DuPont ties together profitability, asset-use efficiency and use of debt by splitting the return ratios into a product:

\[ ROA = \frac{NI}{TA} = \underbrace{\frac{NI}{\text{Sales}}}_{\text{profit margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Sales}}{TA}}_{\text{total asset turnover}} \] \[ ROE = \frac{NI}{CE} = \underbrace{\frac{NI}{\text{Sales}}}_{PM} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Sales}}{TA}}_{TAT} \times \underbrace{\frac{TA}{CE}}_{\text{equity multiplier}} = ROA \times EM \]

  • Useful for quick estimates of how operating changes affect returns, e.g. how much a higher asset turnover raises ROA.
  • If a firm were financed only with common equity, total assets = common equity, EM = 1 and ROA = ROE.
  • Since equity = TA × (1 − debt ratio), \(EM = \dfrac{1}{1 - \text{debt ratio}}\). More debt → bigger EM → higher ROE, and more risk.

Twelve traps that cost marks
  1. “Gross profit margin” in this course is EBIT / Sales.
  2. ROA here uses net income; EBIT / TA is BEP.
  3. Margins, ROA, ROE and EPS use NI minus preferred dividends.
  4. DSO: lecture 365 days, tutorial key 360 days. State which you use.
  5. A loss is not taxed: EBT = −2,000 ⇒ NI = −2,000 (T1 P5).
  6. Volume changes move sales and variable costs, not fixed costs or interest.
  7. “MC = 30% of sales” means variable costs are 70% (T1 P6).
  8. Depreciation sits inside fixed costs but costs no cash: NCF = NI + D.
  9. More depreciation ⇒ lower NI but higher cash flow (tax shield D·t).
  10. Retained earnings are a claim, not cash.
  11. Debt ratio uses total liabilities, current ones included.
  12. Watch for distractors (T2 P8, P11, P12, P13): not every number is needed.
