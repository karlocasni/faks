An Overview of Financial Management

1.1What finance is, and what the financial manager does

  • Finance = managing money, i.e. the financial side of business. Every business transaction begins and ends with a financial one; money is tied up in all assets.
  • Corporate: the principles come from the listed corporation and apply in full only to it, the most complex form.

Five primary activities: (1) cash management, (2) minimising the cost of capital, (3) strategic investment (capital budgeting), (4) allocation of income: dividends vs retained earnings, (5) risk management. They fall into two roles: Treasury and Financial planning & analysis.

  • Corporation = a legal entity created by a state, separate from its owners and managers. Losses are limited to the funds invested; it outlives its founders.
  • Double taxation: earnings are taxed at the corporate level, then dividends are taxed again as the stockholders’ income.
  • Growth path: entrepreneur’s own funds → stock sold to outsiders (friends, family, private investors, venture capitalists) → banks and/or IPO → banks, debt issues, additional shares.
Exam trapEasy transfer and easy capital raising are really true only once the corporation goes public.

1.3The goal: shareholder wealth, and what creates value

Not profit, not survival: the primary goal is long-term shareholder wealth maximisation, i.e. maximising the fundamental stock price (which reflects all relevant information), not just the current market price (investors rarely have all the information). Why not enterprise value? Because of the agency problem (1.4).

Three facts about value: (1) an asset is worth something only if it generates cash flow; (2) timing matters, sooner is better; (3) investors are risk averse. Hence:

\[ \text{Value} = \frac{FCF_1}{(1+WACC)^1} + \frac{FCF_2}{(1+WACC)^2} + \dots + \frac{FCF_\infty}{(1+WACC)^\infty} \]

Managers raise value by (1) increasing the expected cash flows, (2) speeding up their receipt, (3) reducing their risk (lower WACC). This is the risk–return trade-off, faced by managers and investors alike.

SELF-CHECK 1

Which lever?

Name the lever and the effect on value: (a) suppliers now give 60-day instead of 30-day terms; (b) a patent blocks competitors for five more years; (c) one big customer is replaced by fifty smaller ones with the same total sales.
Solution

(a) Timing: cash goes out later, value ↑. (b) Size: bigger, longer-lasting cash flows, value ↑. (c) Risk: cash flows are more certain, WACC ↓, value ↑.

1.4Agency relationships, and what successful firms do

In small firms owners are managers. In large corporations shareholders elect directors, who hire managers. An agency relationship exists whenever a principal hires an agent to act on their behalf. The agency problem is the conflict of interest when agents use their power for their own benefit. It is addressed by corporate governance. It exists between shareholders and managers, shareholders and creditors, majority and minority shareholders, employee-shareholders and other shareholders, and so on.

  • Shareholders vs managers: managers lean toward their own interest; four disciplining factors are compensation plans, direct intervention by shareholders, the threat of firing and the threat of takeover.
  • Shareholders vs creditors: shareholders (through managers) may raise the stock price at creditors’ expense, e.g. with riskier projects. In the long run this raises the cost of debt and lowers the stock price.

Successful firms achieve two goals: (1) deliver products valued more than competitors’ (lower price or better product); (2) sell at prices that cover costs and compensate owners and creditors for risk. Three attributes: skilled people at all levels; strong relationships with outsiders (suppliers, outsourcers); enough capital: retained earnings plus external funds (stock and/or borrowing).

SELF-CHECK 2

Agency in practice

(a) A CEO has a fixed salary and retires in two years. Which agency problem, and which disciplining factor would you strengthen? (b) A heavily indebted firm takes on a very risky project. Who gains, who loses, and what happens to its cost of debt?
Solution

(a) Shareholders vs managers: the CEO gains nothing from long-term value. Tie compensation to the long-term stock price (also board intervention, firing, takeover threat).

(b) Shareholders vs creditors: shareholders keep the upside, creditors bear much of the downside (limited liability). Creditors respond by raising the cost of debt, which lowers the stock price in the long run.

The Financial Environment

2.1Who saves, who borrows, and how capital moves

In the aggregate, individuals are net savers (they provide most funds used by nonfinancial corporations); nonfinancial corporations and governments are net borrowers; financial corporations borrow slightly more than they lend.

Full picture (slide 2-6): the issuer sells new securities in the primary market, directly or via an investment banker (“wholesale”); brokers and dealers (“retail”) pass them on to other investors. Around them: the regulator, government (tax) and information providers.

2.2Financial institutions and markets

Ten institutions: investment banks, brokers & dealers; commercial banks; financial services corporations; savings & loan associations; mutual savings banks; credit unions; and the typical institutional investors: life insurance companies, mutual (investment) funds, pension funds, hedge and private equity funds. In the USA institutions own ~60% of publicly held stock and make ~75% of trades.

2.3The stock market

  • Three transactions: secondary trading of outstanding shares; seasoned (secondary) equity offering = new shares of an already public firm (primary); IPO = new shares of a privately held firm going public (primary).
  • Stock exchange (e.g. NYSE): only members trade; formal, centralised; clearing house (depository, clearing & settlement); transparency (quotes, public reporting).

Exam trapThe company gets money only in the primary market. Shares bought on the exchange from another investor give the company nothing; it still cares because liquidity and the market price matter for future issues.

2.4The cost of money

Capital is allocated through a price system: supply and demand set the cost of money. For debt it is the interest rate; for equity the cost of equity (expected dividends + capital gains). The user’s cost is the provider’s return, so the terms are interchangeable.

\[ r = r_{RF} + r_R = \underbrace{r_{rf} + r_i}_{r_{RF}} + \underbrace{r_{default} + r_{liquidity} + r_{maturity}}_{r_R} \]
rrf
Real risk-free rate: the riskless rate with no inflation, typically 1–4% (USA).
ri
Inflation premium (expected inflation); all market rates include it.
rRF
Nominal risk-free rate = rrf + ri.
rR
Risk premium of the security, from its default risk, liquidity and maturity.

What drives it (slide 2-15): general economic conditions (supply/demand for money, inflation) → rRF; market conditions (marketability of the security), firm risk (business and financial risk) and financing conditions (capital requirements) → rR.

Stack the components. A government bill has (almost) only the first two; a corporate bond adds the three risk premiums.

SELF-CHECK 4

Required return on a bond

rrf = 2%, expected inflation 3%. A 10-year corporate bond has default 1.5%, liquidity 0.5%, maturity 1% premiums. (a) rRF? (b) rR and r? (c) Inflation expectations rise to 4%: effect?
Solution

(a) r_RF = 2% + 3% = 5% (b) r_R = 3% r = 8% (c) The inflation premium is inside every rate, so all required returns rise by 1 point (this bond 9%); risk premiums are unchanged.

2.5Risk structure and term structure

Term structure: the same issuer (same risk) can pay different yields for different maturities. Yield vs maturity = the yield curve; its traditional shape slopes upward. Three theories:

  • Expectations theory: long rates reflect expected future short rates (expected rise → upward curve, expected fall → inverted).
  • Liquidity preference theory: investors demand a premium for longer maturities, so the curve normally slopes up. Variant: preferred habitat, where investors leave their preferred maturity only for a premium.
  • Segmented markets theory: each maturity is a separate market with its own supply and demand.
SELF-CHECK 5

Reading the curves

(a) Why is BB above AAA at every maturity? (b) Short rates are expected to stay flat, yet the Treasury curve slopes up. Which theory explains it?
Solution

(a) Same maturity premium, but BB has a larger default (and liquidity) premium. (b) Liquidity preference: pure expectations would give a flat curve; the slope is the premium for longer maturities.

Financial Statements, Cash Flow and Taxes

3.1The annual report and the balance sheet

The annual report has a verbal section (why things happened) and four statements (what happened): balance sheet, income statement, statement of retained earnings, statement of cash flows. They are audited and mandatory for listed companies (even quarterly).

The balance sheet is a snapshot at one point in time: assets (what is owned) = liabilities + equity (how it is financed), so \(E = A - L\).

  • Assets in order of liquidity (time to convert to cash at fair value); claims in the order they must be paid. Stockholders come last: a residual claim that never has to be “paid off”.
  • Book values (historical, as recorded), not market values (current prices).
Assets
Current assetsheld short term, used “one time”: cash & equivalents, short-term investments, receivables (sold but not yet paid), inventories
Fixed assetsused gradually: physical (land, buildings, equipment; depreciation), long-term investments (e.g. a subsidiary’s stock), intangibles (patents, trademarks, goodwill; amortization)
Liabilities & equity
Current liabilities≤ 1 year: accounts payable (suppliers), notes payable (bank loans), accruals (wages, taxes)
Long-term liabilities> 1 year: bonds, long-term loans
Equityno maturity: (preferred stock) + common stock + retained earnings = common equity (net worth)
Long-term liabilities + equity = the company’s capital (long-term financing)
  • Cash equivalents mature very soon and convert at close to book value; short-term investments have less predictable values.
  • Preferred stock is a hybrid of debt and equity: below debt, above common stock in bankruptcy; fixed dividend, so no gain when earnings grow. Rarely used, so “equity” usually means common equity.
  • Retained earnings = cumulative earnings not paid out as dividends.
  • Working capital: gross WC = current assets; net WC = CA − CL. Focus on net operating WC (operating CA: cash, receivables, inventories). The permanently needed part of current assets should be financed long-term.

3.3Volume, fixed costs and profit

When units change, sales and variable costs change in proportion; fixed costs and interest don’t, so profit moves faster than sales. With q units, price p, unit variable cost v, fixed costs F, interest I:

\[ NI = \big[\,q\,(p - v) - (F + I)\,\big](1 - t) \qquad \text{(only if EBT} > 0) \]
Exam trap · losses are not taxedIf EBT < 0, tax = 0 and NI = EBT (the key to P5: EBT = −2,000 ⇒ NI = −2,000).

3.5Net income ≠ net cash flow

Some revenues and expenses are not received or paid in cash in the same year. Depreciation and amortization are noncash charges: they reduce NI but no one is paid, so add them back:

\[ \text{Net cash flow} = NI + D\&A \qquad (\text{precisely: } NI - \text{noncash revenues} + \text{noncash charges}) \]
  • Other cash-flow concepts: operating cash flow, free cash flow (FCF).
  • Profit needs assumptions (input use, prices); cash flow needs none. But cash comes from profit (the internal funding source).
  • Cash flows and revenues/expenses mismatch in timing, definition and use:

3.6Sources and uses of cash · statement of cash flows

A large NI doesn’t mean more cash at year end: NI can be used in many ways, and cash can come from elsewhere.

Sources (↑ cash)Uses (↓ cash)
Net income + depreciationDividend payments
Increase in long-term debtDecrease in long-term debt
Increase in equityDecrease in equity
Increase in current liabilitiesIncrease in fixed assets

Rule: liability or equity ↑, or non-cash asset ↓ = source; the reverse = use. The statement has three sections: operating (NI, depreciation, changes in current assets and liabilities other than cash, short-term investments and short-term debt); investing (buying and selling fixed assets); financing (issuing short-term debt, long-term debt or stock; dividends; buying back stock or bonds).

Analysis of Financial Statements

4.1Purpose, methods, users, limitations

Ratios show whether the firm is improving or deteriorating and its strengths and weaknesses vs the industry. They let you analyse direction, size and trends of changes; compare with similar firms, the best and worst firm and the industry average; and set or track strategy (planning).

  • Methods: trend analysis (a ratio over time); common-size analysis (income statement ÷ sales, balance sheet ÷ total assets, so firms and years become comparable); percent-change analysis (growth rates of all items).
  • Users: managers (control, improve operations); credit analysts, i.e. banks and bond raters (can it pay its debts?); stock analysts (efficiency, risk, growth).
  • Limitations:
    • Multidivisional firms: no meaningful industry average, so ratios suit small, focused firms better.
    • Inflation distorts balance-sheet values, depreciation, inventory costs and profit; judge firms of different ages carefully.
    • Seasonality: e.g. tourism inventories in vs after the season. Use monthly averages for turnover ratios.
    • Window dressing by management.
    • Different accounting practices: inventory valuation, depreciation, leases.
    • Ratios hard to judge: a high current ratio may mean strong liquidity (good) or excess cash (bad); a high fixed-asset turnover may mean efficiency or too few assets.
    • A mix of good and bad ratios: banks use statistical techniques to conclude.
    • Data accuracy: Enron, WorldCom.

Five groups: liquidity, asset management, debt management, profitability, market value. Profitability is the combined result of the first three; market value is what investors think of it all.

  • Asset-management ratios complement the liquidity ratios: they show how long cash is tied up in each asset. Turnover = Sales / asset item; conversion period = 365 / turnover. Use average asset values where possible.
  • Industry averages aren’t targets, but a big gap is a red flag. Example: a low current ratio due to low inventories could be a strength (just-in-time) or a weakness (missed shipments, lost sales). Ratios point where to look; they don’t answer why.
Exam traps
  1. 360 or 365? Lecture formulas use 365; the Tutorial 2 key uses 360 (P2: DSO 80, P7: 45). Both are shown below; use what your instructor states.
  2. “Gross profit margin” = EBIT / Sales in this course; inventory turnover uses Sales, not COGS.
  3. Debt ratio uses total liabilities. D/E = TL/E is a different ratio (D/E = 1 ⇔ debt ratio 50%).

4.3One-ratio problems

Most are solved by walking the income statement up or down: \(EBT = NI/(1-t)\), \(EBIT = EBT + I\), \(I = EBIT/TIE\), \(TA = EBIT/BEP\).

TUT 2 · P 5

DSO from a percentage

Average accounts receivable equal 20% of sales. What is the DSO?

Solution

\(DSO = \dfrac{0.2\,S}{S/365} = 0.2 \times 365\) 73 days (360-day key convention: 72 days). Sales cancel out.

TIE from net income

Net income 10,500 EUR; annual interest 5,000 EUR; tax 30%. TIE?

Solution

EBT = 10,500 / 0.7 = 15,000; EBIT = 20,000; TIE = 20,000 / 5,000 TIE = 4 key ✓

TUT 2 · P 13

Profit margin 15% on sales of $20,000,000; debt $7,500,000; total assets $22,500,000; after-tax interest cost on debt 5%. ROA?

Interest expense from BEP and ROA

Total assets $100M; tax 40%; BEP 15%; ROA 9%. Interest expense?

Solution

EBIT = 15M; NI = 9M → EBT = 9 / 0.6 = 15M → interest = 15 − 15 $0

Only consistent with no debt: then ROA = BEP(1 − t) = 9% exactly. Same method with ROA = 5%: EBT = 8.33M → interest = $6.67M.

Solution

Interest = 40/8 = 5 → EBT = 35 → NI = 21; TA = 40 / 0.10 = 400 → ROA = 21/400 5.25% key ✓

4.5DuPont analysis

Ties together profitability, asset efficiency and use of debt, so you don’t miss the forest for the trees:

\[ ROA = \underbrace{\frac{NI}{\text{Sales}}}_{\text{profit margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Sales}}{TA}}_{\text{asset turnover}} \qquad ROE = PM \times TAT \times \underbrace{\frac{TA}{CE}}_{\text{equity multiplier}} = ROA \times EM \]
  • Good for quick estimates: e.g. how much higher asset turnover raises ROA.
  • All-equity firm: TA = CE, EM = 1, so ROA = ROE.
  • \(EM = 1/(1 - \text{debt ratio})\): more debt → higher ROE and more risk.
TUT 2 · P 4

Both drivers up 15%

By how many percent does ROA rise if net profit margin and total asset turnover both rise by 15%?

Solution

\(1.15 \times 1.15 = 1.3225\) +32.25%, not 30%: the effects multiply.

Net profit margin 2.7%; TAT 2.4; DSO 45 days; tax 35%; sales 120,000; equity = 40% of assets. ROE?

Solution

EM = 1/0.4 = 2.5 → ROE = 2.7% × 2.4 × 2.5 16.20% key ✓ DSO, tax and sales are distractors.

Twelve traps that cost marks
  1. “Gross profit margin” in this course is EBIT / Sales.
  2. ROA here uses net income; EBIT / TA is BEP.
  3. Margins, ROA, ROE and EPS use NI minus preferred dividends.
  4. DSO: lecture 365 days, tutorial key 360 days. State which you use.
  5. A loss is not taxed: EBT = −2,000 ⇒ NI = −2,000 (T1 P5).
  6. Volume changes move sales and variable costs, not fixed costs or interest.
  7. “MC = 30% of sales” means variable costs are 70% (T1 P6).
  8. Depreciation sits inside fixed costs but costs no cash: NCF = NI + D.
  9. More depreciation ⇒ lower NI but higher cash flow (tax shield D·t).
  10. Retained earnings are a claim, not cash.
  11. Debt ratio uses total liabilities, current ones included.
  12. Watch for distractors (T2 P8, P11, P12, P13): not every number is needed.
